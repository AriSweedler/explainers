The College Football Playoff

Every January one team is named the champion of college football, and every year the way it is chosen seems to change. The system is three systems stacked on one another: leagues of schools that sell television rights, a postseason that was never designed as a whole, and a century of rules about paying the players that the courts have been dismantling since 2014. This article builds the three in order, from a map of the schools to the deals, the brackets and the court cases, until the playoff of 2026 is the obvious result.

The conferences

Start with the schools. A Conference, a league of eight to eighteen schools that play one another, crown a champion and act as one business, is the unit everything else is built from. The league signs the television contract, not the school. It then splits the money among its members. A school chooses a league the way a shop chooses a mall: for the foot traffic and the rent.

Every dot on the map is a school, colored by its league in the 2026 season. Pick SEC and sixteen dots stay red while the rest fade; pick Big Ten and eighteen blue dots run from Los Angeles to New Jersey. The line at the bottom left gives each league's size and what one member took home in 2024-25. The two hollow rings belong to no league at all.

The colors are the whole story in miniature. The Big Ten shared $1.37 billion among 18 schools in 2024-25, so a school's league sets its income more than its own stadium does. That is why the dots move, and the rest of this section is about why they move where they do.

The 138 dots are the Football Bowl Subdivision, the top level of college football, whose leagues and independents run their own postseason instead of a tournament the NCAA runs. The NCAA, the National Collegiate Athletic Association, runs a bracket for every other level of the sport. At the top it only sets the rules. We will shorten the name to FBS.

Four leagues hold 67 schools: the Big Ten with 18, the ACC with 17, and the SEC and Big 12 with 16 each. Six more leagues hold 69, and two schools hold out alone. The split between the first four and the other six is the split that runs through the playoff, the money and the law.

The money arrives through a Media rights deal, a league's multi-year contract that sells its football and basketball broadcasts to networks and streaming services. It is the largest source of money a league shares. The biggest one sold seven years of Big Ten games to Fox, CBS and NBC for over $7 billion, starting in 2023.

The top bar is about $1 billion a year, and no other league's is close. Flip to per school and the gaps shrink but keep their order: the Big Ten paid about $79.9 million to each full-share school in 2024-25, the Big 12 about $39.5 million, the American about $7 million. A school that moves from the bottom of this chart to the top multiplies its income tenfold. No stadium expansion does that.

These deals exist because of one court case, and the leagues that sign them are shaped by it.

Until 1984 no league could sell its games. The NCAA sold every school's football telecasts through a few network contracts and capped how often any team appeared. NCAA v. Board of Regents of the University of Oklahoma, the 1984 Supreme Court decision that this plan violated antitrust law, ended it. Oklahoma and Georgia had sued after the NCAA threatened schools that signed their own deals.

Before the ruling, the one box at the top sells to the networks, and the cap limits every team to six appearances in two years. Move the slider to 1984 and the box becomes seven sellers, with Notre Dame selling alone. The court voted 7-2 on 27 June 1984. From that day a league's television value was its own to grow, and the way to grow it was to add the schools that networks wanted.

Growing by adding schools is Conference realignment, the recurring reshuffling of which schools belong to which league, driven mainly by the size of each league's Media rights deal. It comes in waves. The latest ran from 2021 to 2026 and left one league with two members before it was rebuilt.

Start in 1990: fifteen hollow rings, mostly in the East, and two leagues that no longer exist in Texas and the plains. By 1996 the Southwest Conference is gone and the Big 12 has formed from its Texas schools and the Big Eight. In 2005 the ACC reaches into the Big East for Miami, Virginia Tech and Boston College. In 2014 the Big East's football schools are gone, and Maryland and Rutgers have joined the Big Ten. In 2024 nine schools move in one summer: Texas and Oklahoma to the SEC, USC, UCLA, Oregon and Washington to the Big Ten, Cal, Stanford and SMU to the ACC. The purple dots on the Pacific coast almost vanish.

Each wave follows a contract. A league adds schools when its deal is up for renewal and the networks will pay more for more markets. The schools that move go toward the top bar of the previous chart. We now take the five leagues of the colored dots one at a time.

The Southeastern Conference, a 16-school league based in Birmingham, Alabama, that added Texas and Oklahoma in 2024, is one of the four richest. Its exclusive deal with ESPN began in 2024. We will call it the SEC, as everyone does.

In 1991 the count reads ten. Arkansas and South Carolina arrive in 1992, and the twelve-team league plays the first conference title game in the sport. Texas A&M and Missouri make fourteen; Texas and Oklahoma make sixteen. The league paid its fourteen older members an average of $72.4 million in 2024-25. Every addition was a television market: Arkansas and the Carolinas, then St. Louis and Houston, then the whole state of Texas.

The Big Ten Conference, an 18-school league based in Rosemont, Illinois, that added USC, UCLA, Oregon and Washington in 2024, is the top payer. Its $7 billion deal with Fox, CBS and NBC runs from 2023 to 2030. It is the Big Ten in the prose that follows.

The name was true in 1992. Penn State makes eleven, Nebraska twelve, Maryland and Rutgers fourteen. Then in 2024 the count jumps to eighteen and four blue dots appear on the Pacific. A Midwestern league now spans three time zones. Each full-share school received about $79.9 million in 2024-25; Oregon and Washington took a reduced share that rises each year.

The Atlantic Coast Conference, a league with 17 football schools that added Cal, Stanford and SMU in 2024 and counts Notre Dame as a member in other sports, has an ESPN deal that runs to 2036. It is the ACC from here on. Its payout trails the two leagues above.

Eight schools in 1991, all on the Atlantic coast. Florida State makes nine. The raid on the Big East makes twelve in 2005 and fourteen in 2014. In 2024 the count reads seventeen and two orange dots sit on San Francisco Bay. The fourteen full-share schools averaged $47.1 million in 2024-25, and the three newcomers about $19.9 million. The long contract is a trap as well as a floor: Florida State and Clemson sued to leave it, and settled in 2025 for a declining exit fee.

The Big 12 Conference, a 16-school league based in Irving, Texas, formed in 1994 from the Big Eight and four Texas schools and began play in 1996. It lost Texas and Oklahoma to the SEC in 2024 but added eight schools in 2023 and 2024.

In 1995 the grey dots are two leagues. In 1996 twelve of them turn green. The count falls to ten by 2012 as Nebraska, Colorado, Missouri and Texas A&M leave for richer leagues. Then the league rebuilds outward: BYU, Cincinnati, Houston and UCF in 2023, and Arizona, Arizona State, Colorado and Utah in 2024. It paid full-share schools about $39.5 million in 2024-25, less than the ACC. Where the leagues above grew by taking, this one grew by replacing.

The Pac-12 Conference, the West Coast league founded in 1915 that lost ten of twelve members in 2024 and relaunched on 1 July 2026 with eight football schools, is what replacement looks like from the other side. It now ranks with the second-tier leagues, under deals with CBS and The CW that run through 2030-31.

Ten schools in 2010, twelve in 2011 with Colorado and Utah. Then in 2024 the count reads two. USC and UCLA had announced for the Big Ten in June 2022; the league's best offer to the rest was a streaming deal worth about $20 to 25 million a school, and ten left within fourteen months. In 2026 eight purple dots return, six of them from the Mountain West and one from Texas. The name survived; the money did not.

The four leagues that took the Pac-12's schools are the Power Four, the four richest leagues in the FBS: the ACC, Big Ten, Big 12 and SEC. They write their own athlete-benefit rules. The group was five until the Pac-12 fell to two members in 2024.

From 2026 the Big Ten and SEC take 29% each of the playoff's television money, the ACC 17.1% and the Big 12 14.7%. The four together take 89.8% of about $1.3 billion a year. The same two leagues also hold the final say over the playoff's format from 2026, a fact we return to in the next section.

Everyone else is the Group of Five, the FBS leagues outside the Power Four, such as the Sun Belt and the Mountain West. The rebuilt Pac-12 now counts as a sixth, so the group is also called the Group of Six.

The 69 solid dots share one guaranteed playoff spot and about 9% of the money, roughly $115 million, or about $1.8 million a school. The 67 hollow rings share four guaranteed spots and 89.8%. The two groups play the same sport on the same Saturdays. Membership, not results, sets the split.

Two schools belong to neither group. An FBS independent, a top-level program that belongs to no league for football, builds its own schedule and signs its own Media rights deal. Only Notre Dame and UConn remain in 2026, after BYU, Army and UMass joined leagues.

The grey line starts at fifteen in 1990, when Penn State, Florida State and Miami all stood alone, and drops to eight the next year as the Big East starts playing football. The blue line marks 1991, the first season of Notre Dame's own NBC contract, which it has kept ever since and extended through 2029. By 2026 the line sits at two. Notre Dame sells its own games, reportedly for about $50 million a year, and takes an $18.1 million partial share from the ACC for its other sports. It can afford independence. Nobody else can.

So the schools sort themselves by television value into four rich leagues, six poorer ones and one holdout. The postseason they share was built on top of this sorting, one contract at a time.

The postseason

College football had no championship game for its first ninety years. It had the Bowl game, a one-off postseason game that a local civic or commercial committee runs and that invites two teams after the season ends. The name comes from the bowl-shaped Rose Bowl stadium in Pasadena, opened in 1922. For most of history bowls were exhibitions with no title attached. What fixed their matchups was contracts.

Each line is a contract. The Big Ten champion and the Pac-10 champion go to the Rose Bowl, every year since the 1947 game. The SEC champion goes to the Sugar, the Big Eight champion to the Orange, the Southwest champion to the Cotton. Everyone else is invited at large. Flip to 1991: No. 2 Washington is bound to the Rose Bowl by contract, No. 1 Miami takes an at-large place in the Orange Bowl, and the two never meet. Both win. The contracts, not the rankings, decided who played whom.

So the champion was voted, not won. The AP Poll, the weekly Top 25 that the Associated Press has compiled since 1936 from the votes of about 62 sportswriters and broadcasters, named one champion in its final poll. A second poll of coaches, begun in 1950, named another. When the two disagreed, the season ended with two champions.

The left panel is the sportswriters' choice and the right panel the coaches'. In 1954, the first split, Ohio State and UCLA each finished first. In 1991 it is the Miami and Washington of the previous figure. In 2003 the coaches were bound by contract to vote for the winner of the official title game, LSU, and the sportswriters chose USC anyway. The polls have disagreed eleven times since 1950. Every fix that follows is an attempt to make the two best teams play each other.

The first fix was the Bowl Coalition, the 1992-1994 agreement among five leagues, Notre Dame and seven bowls to send the two highest-ranked teams to one bowl as a title game. The Big Ten and Pac-10 stayed out because their champions were locked into the Rose Bowl.

The six members pool the four bowls in the dashed box: whichever bowl holds the No. 1 champion gets No. 2 as well. The Rose Bowl sits outside the box with its two champions. Flip to 1994: No. 1 Nebraska, 12-0, plays inside the pool and beats Miami; No. 2 Penn State, 11-0, plays in the Rose Bowl and beats Oregon. Two unbeaten teams, two bowls, one more split. A three-bowl successor pact ran from 1995 to 1997 with the same hole, and 1997 split again between Michigan and Nebraska.

The hole closed when Pasadena agreed to release its champions when a title game needed them. The result was the Bowl Championship Series, the 1998-2013 system that used human polls and computer formulas to pair the top two teams in one title game. It grew out of the Bowl Coalition and finally brought the Big Ten, the Pac-10 and the Rose Bowl inside. We call it the BCS.

From 1998 to 2003 the standings added a poll average, a computer average, strength of schedule, a point per loss and, from 2001, a bonus for quality wins; the lowest total was best. In 2003 that sum put LSU and Oklahoma in the title game and left the AP's No. 1, USC, outside it, the split you saw two figures ago. From 2004 the formula became one poll, a second poll and the average of six computer rankings, each a third. The title game rotated among four bowls through 2005 and then stood alone from the 2006 season. It still chose exactly two teams. In 2004 an unbeaten Auburn was the third, and in 2011 the two were Alabama and LSU, who had already played each other.

Two teams were not enough, so the formula gave way to a committee. The College Football Playoff, the single-elimination tournament that has decided the FBS champion since the 2014 season, replaced the BCS. A 13-member committee picks and orders the field. It had four teams through 2023 and twelve from 2024. ESPN paid about $470 million a year for the first twelve seasons.

With four teams the bracket is two semifinals and a final: three games. Alabama, Clemson, Georgia and Ohio State filled 22 of the 40 places in ten years. With twelve, the top four seeds skip a round and eight teams play a first round on campus: eleven games. In 2024 the five highest-ranked league champions got in automatically and the four highest-ranked of those took the byes, so No. 9 Boise State and No. 12 Arizona State rested while higher-ranked teams played. The committee changed that for 2025, seeding straight down its rankings. Ohio State won the first twelve-team bracket as an 8 seed, beating Notre Dame 34-23 on 20 January 2025; Indiana won the second as the 1 seed, beating Miami 27-21 on 19 January 2026.

The bowls did not disappear; they became rounds. The New Year's Six, the six bowl games under contract to the College Football Playoff, are the Rose, Sugar, Orange, Cotton, Fiesta and Peach. In the four-team era two of them hosted the semifinals each year; in the twelve-team bracket all six host playoff games.

In 2014 the Rose and Sugar turn blue; in 2015 Orange and Cotton; in 2016 Fiesta and Peach. The three pairs repeat for nine seasons. From 2024 the other four turn green as quarterfinals, and the Rose Bowl keeps its 1 January date whatever round it holds. In 2026 the pairs break: Orange and Sugar host the semifinals, on 14 and 15 January 2027. A bowl is now a venue with a date, and the committee fills it.

Twelve is not the end. A 16-team format, a College Football Playoff bracket of sixteen, is the expansion the SEC favored for 2026 and later. The Big Ten pushed for 24 instead, and the deadlock kept the twelve-team bracket for 2026.

The twelve-team bracket is the one in force, with a new rule for 2026: each Power Four champion is in whatever its ranking, the best Group of Six team is in, and Notre Dame is in if it is ranked in the top twelve. The sixteen-team bracket is the SEC's 5+11: five champions and eleven at-large teams, no byes, fifteen games. The 24-team bracket is the Big Ten's 23 best plus one, with eight byes and two campus rounds in December that would replace league title games. The Big Ten and SEC hold the final say over the format under the 2026-2031 contract, which pays about $1.3 billion a year, and neither yielded by the 23 January 2026 deadline. The committee discussed the 24-team model again on 30 September 2026 without a vote. It has until 1 December 2026 to change the 2027 format.

The bracket, then, is whatever the two richest leagues agree on. What they may give the players who fill it is the last part of the system, and the part the courts rewrote.

Paying the players

For sixty years the rule was simple. Amateurism, the principle that college athletes compete for an education and may not be paid to play, set a ceiling on what a school could give. Courts narrowed it in a series of antitrust cases, and since July 2025 schools may pay athletes directly. We start at the ceiling.

Before 1956 the NCAA's rule allowed only need-based aid, and the schools ignored it. In 1956 the NCAA allowed a scholarship for athletic ability: the grey box, tuition, fees, room, board and books. The dashed box above it is everything else, and it stayed empty through 2014. The NCAA's own lawyers coined "student-athlete" in the 1950s to keep injured players from being employees for workers' compensation. The scholarship was the whole wage, and it was fixed by rule across every school.

A fixed wage set by agreement among competitors is what antitrust law forbids, and the first case to say so about players was O'Bannon v. NCAA, the 2009 lawsuit by former UCLA basketball player Ed O'Bannon over unpaid use of athletes' likenesses in video games and broadcasts. He had found his own unnamed avatar in an EA Sports game. The court ruled against the NCAA in 2014 and the appeals court trimmed the remedy in 2015.

Before 2014 the scholarship stopped short of what it actually costs to attend: travel, laundry, a phone. On 8 August 2014 Judge Claudia Wilken allowed schools to pay the full cost of attendance and to hold up to $5,000 a year in trust for each player. On 30 September 2015 the Ninth Circuit kept the first and struck the second. The rich leagues had already voted the cost-of-attendance stipend in for 2015. The ceiling had moved a few thousand dollars, and the court had said the ceiling was subject to antitrust law at all.

The Supreme Court said the same, unanimously, six years later. NCAA v. Alston, the 2021 decision that caps on education-linked benefits for athletes violate antitrust law, covered only education-linked pay. It removed Amateurism as a legal shield against paying players at all.

Before the ruling every item on the list is capped or banned by NCAA rule: computers, instruments, tutoring, study abroad, graduate scholarships, paid internships. On 21 June 2021 the court voted 9-0 that the NCAA may not cap them, and schools may now pay up to $5,980 a year in academic awards. Justice Kavanaugh wrote separately that the rest of the NCAA's pay rules "raise serious questions" and ended with "The NCAA is not above the law." Nine days later the NCAA gave up on the next item.

That item was Name, image and likeness, the commercial value of an athlete's identity, which outside parties pay for through endorsements, appearances, social media posts and autographs. College athletes could not earn it until 1 July 2021. It is distinct from money the school itself pays. We shorten it to NIL.

In 2020 the only arrow into the player is the scholarship from the school; the dashed lines from brands and boosters are forbidden. Florida's state law was due to take effect on 1 July 2021 and several states followed, so on 30 June the NCAA adopted an interim policy for the next day. From 1 July 2021 the orange arrows are live. Within months boosters pooled their money into collectives that paid players for appearances, and in February 2024 a federal judge barred the NCAA from policing NIL offers made during recruiting. The school still paid only the scholarship. The money had simply found a door beside it.

Money that follows a player also follows him to a new school. The NCAA transfer portal, an online database launched on 15 October 2018 that lists athletes who have told their school they intend to transfer, lets coaches at other schools contact them once listed. It made transferring a yearly open market.

In 2017 a football player who changes schools sits out a season, and his old school can block other coaches from calling. In 2018 the portal opens the phone lines but the season on the bench remains. On 15 April 2021 a first transfer plays at once; a second still sits. After a court order in December 2023, the NCAA made every transfer in good academic standing eligible at once in April 2024. Put the portal beside NIL and a roster became a one-year contract renegotiated each winter.

The school itself was still barred from paying. The House settlement, the court-approved agreement effective 1 July 2025 that settled three antitrust lawsuits by athletes over pay, ended that. It pays about $2.8 billion to past athletes and lets schools pay current athletes directly, starting at about $20.5 million per school a year.

Grant House, an Arizona State swimmer, and Sedona Prince, a basketball player, sued in June 2020 over the NIL and broadcast money athletes had been denied. The NCAA and the power leagues agreed to settle on 23 May 2024, and Judge Wilken, the same judge as in O'Bannon, approved the deal on 6 June 2025. The NCAA pays about $1.1 billion from reserves; the five power leagues and the other 27 Division I leagues pay the rest through withheld distributions. The money goes to athletes who played from 2016 to 2024, over ten years. The back pay settled the past. The second half of the deal rewrote the future.

That half is Revenue sharing, direct payment of athletes from a school's own athletic budget, which the House settlement allowed from 1 July 2025. Unlike NIL money from outside parties, it comes from the school, up to $20.5 million per school in 2025-26.

The cap is a formula: 22% of the average shared revenue of a power-league school, which came to $20.5 million for 2025-26, so the average school's shared revenue is about $93 million. It rises 4% a year between recalculations, to about $21.3 million in 2026-27 and about $30 million by 2035-36. A school may spread the money across any sports it likes, and most of it goes to football. The scholarship that was the whole wage in 2014 is now the floor under a payroll. What the cap does not cover is the money that still comes from outside, and that needed a referee.

The referee is the College Sports Commission, the enforcement body, led by CEO Bryan Seeley, that the power leagues launched on 6 June 2025 to police the House settlement. It polices the yearly cap on Revenue sharing, enforces roster limits, and reviews outside NIL deals.

Every outside deal of $600 or more is reported to NIL Go, a clearinghouse built by Deloitte, within five business days. A deal with an ordinary business is recorded and cleared. A deal with a collective or a big booster, an associated entity, must pass two tests: a valid business purpose, meaning the player promotes something sold to the public, and pay within the range of similar deals. Otherwise it is not cleared, and the player may revise it, cancel it or take it to arbitration. The commission's first guidance in July 2025 read as a ban on collectives; it was revised within the month. A class action filed in June 2026 challenges the whole clearinghouse, a federal employment case says players may be employees, and a Senate bill to write the cap into law passed on 28 September 2026 and awaits the House. The system is one year old and already in court.

So the player's pay runs through three doors: the school's capped payroll, outside deals checked by a clearinghouse, and a scholarship that no longer needs a court's permission to exceed. Each door was opened by a case the NCAA lost.

Final words

Three systems, one result. Television money sorts the schools into leagues; the two richest leagues set the playoff's shape and take most of its money; and the players, after a decade of lost cases, are paid from the same money through a capped payroll and a clearinghouse. None of it was designed. Each piece is a contract or a court order laid over the last one, and the next piece, a 24-team bracket or a federal law, will be laid the same way.

A league of schools, usually eight to eighteen, that play one another every season, crown a champion, and act as one business. The league sells its members' television rights and splits the money, and a school that belongs to no league is an independent.

The top level of college football, with 138 schools in 2026, whose conferences and independents run their own postseason. It is the only level of college football whose champion is not decided by a tournament that the national governing body runs.
