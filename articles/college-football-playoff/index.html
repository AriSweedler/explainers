The College Football Playoff

Every January one team is named the champion of college football, and every year the way it is chosen seems to change. The system is three systems stacked on one another: conferences of schools that sell television rights, a postseason that was never designed as a whole, and a century of rules about paying the players that the courts have been dismantling since 2014. The first tab is the system as it stands in 2026; the second is how it got this way, one dated entry at a time.

The schools and their conferences

Start with the schools. A Conference is a group of eight to eighteen schools that play one another, crown a champion and act as one business. The conference signs the television contract, not the school. It then splits the money among its members. Ten conferences and two unattached schools cover the top level of the sport in 2026.

Every dot on the map is a school, colored by its conference in the 2026 season; the badge at the upper right names the one you picked. Pick the Southeastern Conference, the SEC, and sixteen dots stand out in red while the rest shrink. Pick the Big Ten and eighteen blue dots run from Los Angeles to New Jersey. The bar at the bottom left is the money that conference paid out in 2024-25, cut into one slice per member. The Big Ten's bar is $1.37 billion in eighteen slices; the American bar is a sliver and the Mountain West has none to show. The two hollow rings belong to no conference. A school's conference sets its income, so schools change conferences; how they moved is the first half of the history.

The 138 dots are the Football Bowl Subdivision, or FBS, the top level of college football, whose conferences and independents run their own postseason. The NCAA, the National Collegiate Athletic Association, runs a bracket for every other level of the sport. At the top it only sets the rules.

In 2026 four conferences hold 67 schools: the Big Ten with 18, the Atlantic Coast Conference, the ACC, with 17, and the SEC and Big 12 with 16 each. Six more conferences hold 69, and two schools hold out alone. Drag back to 1990: the grey bar of conferences that no longer exist holds 38 schools, and fifteen schools belong to none. The split between the first four and the other six runs through the playoff, the money and the law.

The Southeastern Conference, or SEC, is a 16-school conference based in Birmingham, Alabama, that added Texas and Oklahoma in 2024. It is one of the four richest. Its exclusive deal with ESPN began in 2024.

The rows arrive in four cohorts: ten schools by 1933, Arkansas and South Carolina in 1992, Texas A&M and Missouri in 2012, Texas and Oklahoma in 2024. Fourteen red bars end on the $72.4 million line, the average payout to the older members in 2024-25. Texas and Oklahoma are grey stubs: in their first year they took home only playoff and bowl money. Flip to whole league and the bars become one $1.03 billion bar, fourteen equal slices and a sliver. The conference played the first conference title game in the sport, in 1992.

The Big Ten Conference is an 18-school conference based in Rosemont, Illinois, that added USC, UCLA, Oregon and Washington in 2024. It is the top payer. Its $7 billion deal with Fox, CBS and NBC runs from 2023 to 2030.

The name was true in 1992. Penn State makes eleven, Nebraska twelve, Maryland and Rutgers fourteen. In 2024 the count jumps to eighteen and four dots land on the Pacific: USC and UCLA in blue, Oregon and Washington in orange. A Midwestern conference now spans three time zones. The full-share bar reads about $79.9 million on average for 2024-25; Oregon and Washington take a reduced share until 2030.

The Atlantic Coast Conference, or ACC, has 17 football schools, added Cal, Stanford and SMU in 2024, and counts Notre Dame as a member in other sports. Its ESPN deal runs to 2036. Its payout trails the SEC and the Big Ten.

In 1991 the conference has eight schools, all on the Atlantic coast. Florida State makes nine. The raid on the Big East makes twelve in 2005 and fourteen in 2014. In 2024 the count reads seventeen and two small orange dots sit on San Francisco Bay. The fourteen full-share schools averaged $47.1 million in 2024-25; Cal, Stanford and SMU about $19.9 million, which is why their dots are small. Clemson, the high, took $55.1 million, and Notre Dame, with no football membership, $18.1 million. Florida State and Clemson sued to leave the 2036 contract and settled in 2025 for a declining exit fee.

The Big 12 Conference is a 16-school conference based in Irving, Texas. It formed in 1994 from the Big Eight and four Texas schools and began play in 1996. It lost Texas and Oklahoma to the SEC in 2024 but added eight schools in 2023 and 2024.

In 1995 the grey dots are two conferences. In 1996 twelve of them turn green. The count falls to ten by 2012: Nebraska leaves a blue ring where it stood, Colorado a purple one, Missouri and Texas A&M red ones. Then the conference rebuilds outward: BYU, Cincinnati, Houston and UCF in 2023, and Arizona, Arizona State, Colorado and Utah in 2024, the year Texas and Oklahoma leave red rings of their own. Five of the twelve founders were taken by richer conferences. The three conferences above grew by taking. This one grew by replacing.

The Pac-12 Conference is the West Coast conference founded in 1915. It lost ten of twelve members in 2024 and relaunched on 1 July 2026 with eight football schools. It now ranks with the second-tier conferences, under deals with CBS and The CW that run through 2030-31.

The count reads ten in 2010 and twelve in 2011, with Colorado and Utah. In 2024 it reads two, and ten arrows leave the coast: USC and Washington to the Big Ten, Stanford to the ACC, Colorado to the Big 12, each arrow pointing at the nearest school already in its new conference. Only Oregon State and Washington State stay purple. In 2026 six ringed newcomers join them: Boise State and four more from the Mountain West, and Texas State from the Sun Belt. Why ten left in fourteen months is in the history.

The four richest conferences are the Power Four: the ACC, Big Ten, Big 12 and SEC. They write their own athlete-benefit rules. The group was five until the Pac-12 fell to two members in 2024. Everyone else is the Group of Five, the FBS conferences outside the Power Four, such as the Sun Belt and the Mountain West; the rebuilt Pac-12 now counts as a sixth, so the group is also called the Group of Six. Two schools belong to neither group. An FBS independent is a top-level program that belongs to no conference for football, so it builds its own schedule and signs its own television deal. Only Notre Dame and UConn remain in 2026, after BYU, Army and UMass joined conferences.

The money

The money arrives through a Media rights deal, a conference's multi-year contract that sells its football and basketball broadcasts to networks and streaming services. It is the largest source of money a conference shares. The biggest one sold seven years of Big Ten games to Fox, CBS and NBC for over $7 billion, starting in 2023. Conferences can sell their own games because of NCAA v. Board of Regents of the University of Oklahoma, the 1984 Supreme Court decision that the NCAA's plan to control football television violated antitrust law; the case and what it set loose are in the history.

The top bar is $1,370 million, the Big Ten's payout for 2024-25, and most of it is that one deal. The right panel is the same money after the split: a Big Ten full share was about $79.9 million, a Big 12 share about $39.5 million, an American share about $6 million. A school that moves from the bottom of this chart to the top multiplies its income more than tenfold. That is why schools move, and Conference realignment, the recurring reshuffling of which schools belong to which conference, follows each conference's deal; its waves, from 1991 to 2026, are drawn in the history.

The playoff has its own television money, and the Power Four take most of it.

Under the old deal the playoff paid about $470 million a year and split it by a formula that favored no conference much. Under the new deal, from 2026, the pool is about $1.3 billion a year and the Big Ten and SEC bars run far past their dashed outlines: 29% each. The ACC takes 17.1% and the Big 12 14.7%. The four together take 89.8%. The Group of Five bar stops short of its outline. The Big Ten and SEC also hold the final say over the playoff's format from 2026.

At one dot each the map is the one you have seen: 138 schools on the same Saturdays. Drag to sized by money. Ohio State balloons, like every Power Four dot, to about $21 million a year of playoff money. Toledo, 130 miles north, stays a pinprick: the 69 schools of the six other conferences share one guaranteed playoff spot and about 9% of the money, roughly $115 million, under $2 million a school. Membership, not results, sets the split.

Notre Dame stays independent and keeps two bars: its own NBC contract, in place since 1991 and extended through 2029, and an $18.1 million partial share from the ACC for its other sports. Flip to joins the ACC: the NBC bar goes to the conference, and Notre Dame's total falls to the $47.1 million full share every other ACC school gets. UConn has no comparable contract.

The playoff

The College Football Playoff is the single-elimination tournament that has decided the FBS champion since the 2014 season. A 13-member committee picks and orders the field. It had four teams through 2023 and twelve from 2024. It replaced the Bowl Championship Series, or BCS, the 1998-2013 system that used human polls and computer formulas to pair the top two teams in one title game; before the BCS the champion was voted, by the AP Poll, the weekly Top 25 that the Associated Press has compiled since 1936, and by a poll of coaches. How a vote became a bracket is the second half of the history.

The 2014 bracket is two semifinals and a final, three games: Alabama, Oregon, Florida State and Ohio State. Alabama, Clemson, Georgia and Ohio State filled 22 of the 40 places in the ten four-team years. The 2024 bracket has eleven games. The five highest-ranked conference champions got in automatically, and the four best of those took the byes: so No. 9 Boise State and No. 12 Arizona State rested while higher-ranked teams played a first round on campus. Ohio State, the 8 seed, won it all, beating Notre Dame 34-23 on 20 January 2025. The playoff's management committee, the ten conference commissioners and Notre Dame's athletic director, changed the rule for 2025 and seeded straight down the rankings. Indiana won that bracket as the 1 seed, beating Miami 27-21 on 19 January 2026.

The rounds are played in bowls. A Bowl game is a one-off postseason game that a local civic or commercial committee runs and that invites two teams after the season ends; for most of history bowls were exhibitions with no title attached, and contracts fixed their matchups. The New Year's Six are the six bowl games under contract to the College Football Playoff: the Rose, Sugar, Orange, Cotton, Fiesta and Peach. In the four-team era two of them hosted the semifinals each year. In the twelve-team playoff all six host playoff games.

In 2014 the hand points at Rose and Sugar. It turns a third of a circle in 2015 to Orange and Cotton, and again in 2016 to Fiesta and Peach, then comes round to where it began. The year labels stack three deep beside each pair. From 2024 the four bowls off the hand turn green as quarterfinals, and the Rose Bowl keeps its 1 January date through at least the 2027 season. In 2026 the hand breaks the pattern and points at Orange and Sugar, on 14 and 15 January 2027. A bowl is now a venue with a date, and the committee fills it.

A 16-team format is a College Football Playoff bracket of sixteen, the expansion the SEC favored for 2026 and later. The Big Ten pushed for 24 instead. The deadlock kept the twelve-team bracket for 2026.

The twelve-team bracket is the one in force, with three new rules for 2026. Each Power Four champion is in, whatever its ranking. The best Group of Six team is in. Notre Dame is in if it is ranked in the top twelve. Count the marks: five automatic slots and seven at-large. The sixteen-team bracket is the SEC's 5+11: the same five automatic slots, eleven at-large, no byes, fifteen games. The 24-team bracket is the Big Ten's 23 best plus one: one automatic slot, 23 at-large, eight byes, and two campus rounds in December that would replace conference title games.

The Big Ten and SEC hold the final say over the format under the 2026-2031 contract, which pays about $1.3 billion a year. Neither yielded by the 23 January 2026 deadline. The management committee discussed the 24-team model again on 30 September 2026 without a vote. It has until 1 December 2026 to change the 2027 format. The bracket is whatever the Big Ten and SEC agree on.

Paying the players

Until 2025 a school could give a player a scholarship and nothing more. That rule was Amateurism, the principle that college athletes compete for an education and may not be paid to play. Three antitrust cases narrowed it: O'Bannon v. NCAA, the 2009 lawsuit over unpaid use of athletes' likenesses that won them the full cost of attendance in 2015; NCAA v. Alston, the unanimous 2021 Supreme Court decision that caps on education-linked benefits violate antitrust law; and the House settlement, the court-approved agreement, effective 1 July 2025, that settled three antitrust lawsuits, pays about $2.8 billion to past athletes and lets schools pay current athletes directly. The cases are told in order in the history. What they left in place is three doors.

The first door is Revenue sharing, direct payment of athletes from a school's own athletic budget, which the House settlement allowed from 1 July 2025. It comes from the school, up to $20.5 million per school in 2025-26.

The cap is a formula. The whole bar is 22% of the average of eight revenue lines, from tickets to media rights, at a power-conference school: about $93 million in 2025-26. The red part is the cap, $20.5 million; the rest the school keeps. It rises 4% a year between recalculations, to about $21.3 million in 2026-27; the dashed line is the first year's cap, left behind. Projections put it above $30 million a decade on. A school may spread the money across any sports it likes. The scholarship that was the whole wage in 2014 is now the floor under a payroll.

The second door is Name, image and likeness, or NIL, the commercial value of an athlete's identity, which outside parties pay for through endorsements, appearances, social media posts and autographs. College athletes could not earn it until 1 July 2021. It is distinct from money the school itself pays.

In 2020 the only arrow into the player is the scholarship from the school. The red dashed lines from brands and from boosters, the wealthy donors who fund athletic programs, are forbidden. Drag to 2021 and the brand and booster arrows go live: Florida's state law took effect on 1 July 2021, several states followed, and the NCAA adopted an interim policy the day before. In 2022 a third payer appears: collectives, fan-funded groups that pool booster money and pay players for appearances. By 2024 the last dashed line goes solid: in February 2024 Judge Clifton Corker barred the NCAA from policing NIL offers made to recruits.

Outside money needs a referee, and the third door has one. The College Sports Commission is the enforcement body, led by CEO Bryan Seeley, that the power conferences launched on 6 June 2025 to police the House settlement. It polices the yearly cap on Revenue sharing, enforces roster limits, and reviews outside NIL deals.

A deal under $600 goes straight to the player. The athlete reports every deal of $600 or more to NIL Go within five business days. Deloitte built NIL Go as the clearinghouse for these deals. A deal with a brand, an ordinary business, is recorded and cleared. A deal with a booster or a collective, which the settlement calls an associated entity, is routed through two tests. The player must promote something sold to the public, and the pay must fall within the range of similar deals. Pass, and the money is cleared; fail, and the deal is not cleared, and the player may revise it, cancel it or take it to arbitration. The commission's first guidance, in July 2025, read as a ban on collectives; it was revised within the month. A class action filed in June 2026 challenges the clearinghouse itself. Johnson v. NCAA, a federal case in Pennsylvania, argues that players are employees. The Protect College Sports Act, which would write the cap into federal law, passed the Senate 77-22 on 28 September 2026 and awaits the House.

Money that follows a player also follows him to a new school. The NCAA transfer portal is an online database, launched on 15 October 2018, that lists athletes who have told their school they intend to transfer. Once an athlete is listed, coaches at other schools may contact them. It made transferring a yearly open market.

Under the rules of 2017 the player sits out his second season and his fourth, one for each transfer, and his old school can block other coaches from calling; the count of seasons played reads two. In 2018 the portal opens the phone lines, but the seasons on the bench remain. From 15 April 2021 the first transfer plays at once; the second still sits. A federal court in West Virginia blocked that limit in December 2023, and in April 2024 the NCAA made every transfer in good academic standing eligible at once: four seasons played. Put the portal beside NIL and a roster became a one-year contract renegotiated each winter. So the player's pay runs through three doors: the school's capped payroll, outside deals checked by a clearinghouse, and a scholarship that no longer needs a court's permission to exceed. Two cases the NCAA lost and one it settled opened them.

How it got this way

Every rule on the other tab is a repair laid over an older one. The entries below run from the first bowl contract to the 2026 deadlock. Each opens to the full account and, where one exists, the figure that shows it.

  1. 1947 The Rose Bowl signs the Big Ten and the Pac-10, and contracts start deciding who plays whom.

    College football had no title game until 1992. It had the Bowl game, an exhibition a local committee runs; the name comes from the bowl-shaped Rose Bowl stadium in Pasadena. Contracts fixed the matchups: from the 1947 game the Rose Bowl took the Big Ten and Pac-10 champions every year, the Sugar took the SEC champion, the Orange the Big Eight's, the Cotton the Southwest's. Everyone else was invited at large.

    Each grey line is a contract: Big Ten and Pac-10 to the Rose, SEC to the Sugar, Big Eight to the Orange, Southwest to the Cotton; everyone else at large. Pick 1991: a contract binds No. 2 Washington to the Rose Bowl, No. 1 Miami takes an at-large place in the Orange Bowl, and the two never meet. Both win. Step back through 1990, 1988 and 1986: in four of these six seasons the two thick lines land in different bowls.

  2. 1954 The two polls name two champions for the first time; they would disagree eleven times.

    So the champion was voted, not won. The AP Poll's final vote named one champion; a poll of coaches, begun in 1950, named another. When they disagreed, the season ended with two champions.

    Every season from 1950 to 2013 is a grey dot on the strip. As the orange cursor passes a split season, the dot forks: the sportswriters' champion above, the coaches' champion below. The first fork is 1954: Ohio State above, UCLA below. In 1991 it is Miami and Washington. In 2003 the coaches were bound by contract to vote for the winner of the official title game, LSU, and the sportswriters chose USC anyway. By 2013 the tally reads eleven forks in 64 seasons.

  3. 1956 The NCAA allows the athletic scholarship, and for 59 years it is the whole wage.

    Before 1956 the NCAA allowed only need-based aid. In 1956 it allowed a scholarship for athletic ability: tuition, fees, room, board and books. Everything else stayed forbidden under Amateurism. Walter Byers, the NCAA's executive director, coined "student-athlete" in the 1950s to keep injured players from counting as employees. The scholarship was the whole wage, and the NCAA set the same amount at every school.

    Before 1956 the stack holds only need-based aid. In 1956 the grey box appears, and the dashed box above it, everything else, stays empty while the counter runs to 59 years at 2014. Run the slider on and the stack grows: a cost-of-attendance rung in 2015, an education rung and, beside the stack, outside money in 2021, and a revenue-share rung in 2025. The entries from 2009 on are those rungs, one at a time.

  4. 1984 NCAA v. Board of Regents frees conferences to sell their own television.

    Until 1984 no conference could sell its games. The NCAA sold every school's football telecasts through a few network contracts and capped how often any team appeared. NCAA v. Board of Regents ended it: Oklahoma and Georgia had sued after the NCAA threatened schools that signed their own deals, and the Supreme Court voted 7-2 on 27 June 1984 that the plan violated antitrust law.

    In 1983 the one wide box sells for every school, and the cap limits each team to six appearances in two years. At 1984 the box splits in two: the Big Ten and Pac-10 sell together, and the College Football Association, a bloc of sixty-odd other schools, sells for the rest. In 1991 Notre Dame breaks off to sell alone to NBC. In 1996 the SEC leaves for its own contract, and by 1997 the bloc is gone and every conference sells for itself. From that day a conference's television value was its own to grow, and the way to grow it was to add the schools that networks wanted.

  5. 1992 The Bowl Coalition pairs No. 1 and No. 2 when the Rose Bowl allows; the SEC plays the first conference title game.

    The first fix was the Bowl Coalition, a 1992-1994 pact among five conferences, Notre Dame and seven bowls to pair the two highest-ranked teams in one bowl. The Big Ten and Pac-10 stayed out because their champions were locked into the Rose Bowl. The same year the twelve-team SEC, newly joined by Arkansas and South Carolina, played the first conference title game in the sport.

    The six members pool the bowls in the dashed box: whichever bowl holds the No. 1 champion gets No. 2 as well. The Rose Bowl sits outside with its two champions. In 1992 the two lines meet in one bowl. Drag to 1994: No. 1 Nebraska, 12-0, plays inside the pool and beats Miami, while No. 2 Penn State, 11-0, plays in the Rose Bowl and beats Oregon; the dashed bracket marks the miss, and the polls split again. A three-bowl successor pact ran from 1995 to 1997 with the same hole, and 1997 split between Michigan and Nebraska.

  6. 1996 The Southwest Conference dissolves; the Big 12 forms from its Texas schools and the Big Eight.

    Arkansas had left the Southwest Conference for the SEC in 1991 and SMU's 1987 shutdown had weakened it. In February 1994 Texas, Texas A&M, Texas Tech and Baylor agreed to merge with the Big Eight as the Big 12, playing from 1996; Rice, SMU and TCU went to the Western Athletic Conference and Houston to Conference USA. It was the first time a major conference dissolved because its most valuable members found a richer home. See the 1996 state of the realignment map under the 2011 entry.

  7. 1998 The BCS: a formula picks two teams for one title game, and the Rose Bowl joins.

    The hole closed when Pasadena agreed to release its champions when a title game needed them. The result was the BCS, which grew out of the Bowl Coalition and brought the Big Ten, the Pac-10 and the Rose Bowl inside.

    Under the 1998-2003 formula the standings added five unlike numbers: a poll average, a computer average, strength of schedule, a point per loss and, from 2001, a deduction for quality wins; the lowest total was best. The bars are the final 2003 standings: Oklahoma and LSU above the title-game line, and USC, the AP's No. 1, below it. From 2004 the formula became one poll, a second poll and the average of six computer rankings, each exactly a third. The bars become the final 2004 standings, and the team below the line is Auburn, unbeaten, the third of three. The title game rotated among four bowls through 2005 and then stood alone from the 2006 season. It still chose two teams; in 2011 the two were Alabama and LSU, who had already played each other that season.

  8. 2005 The ACC raids the Big East for Miami, Virginia Tech and Boston College.

    The ACC reached north into the Big East: Miami and Virginia Tech in 2004, Boston College in 2005, twelve schools and a conference title game of its own. The Big East backfilled with Louisville, Cincinnati and South Florida. See the 2005 state of the realignment map under the 2011 entry.

  9. 2009 O'Bannon v. NCAA: a UCLA player finds his avatar in a video game, and antitrust law reaches the scholarship.

    A fixed wage set by agreement among competitors is what antitrust law forbids, and the case that said so about players was O'Bannon v. NCAA. Ed O'Bannon had found his own unnamed avatar in an EA Sports game. On 8 August 2014 Judge Claudia Wilken ruled against the NCAA; on 30 September 2015 the Ninth Circuit trimmed the remedy.

    Before 2014 the limit sits on top of the scholarship, which stopped short of the cost of attendance: travel, laundry, a phone. The 2014 ruling allowed schools to pay the full cost of attendance and to hold up to $5,000 a year in trust for each player; the limit jumps two rungs. The 2015 appeal kept the first remedy and struck the second; the limit settles one rung up. The major conferences adopted the cost-of-attendance stipend in 2015. The limit had moved a few thousand dollars, and the Ninth Circuit had said the limit was subject to antitrust law.

  10. 2011 to 2014 Nebraska, Colorado, Utah, Texas A&M, Missouri, Maryland and Rutgers change conferences; the Big East's football schools scatter.

    Conference realignment comes in waves, each following a contract. Nebraska went to the Big Ten and Colorado and Utah to the Pac-12 in 2011; Texas A&M and Missouri to the SEC in 2012; Pitt, Syracuse and Louisville to the ACC and Maryland and Rutgers to the Big Ten by 2014. The Big East's football schools reorganized as the American.

    Start in 1990: fifteen hollow rings, and two grey conferences in Texas and the plains that no longer exist. By 1996 the Southwest Conference is gone and the Big 12 has formed. By 2005 the ACC has taken Miami, Virginia Tech and Boston College. In 2014 the Big East's football schools are gone, and Maryland and Rutgers have joined the Big Ten. In 2024 the count of arrows reads thirteen in one summer, nine of them to the SEC, Big Ten and ACC, and the Pac-12 count drops from twelve to two. The SEC added Texas and Oklahoma in 2024, the year its ESPN deal began; the Big Ten added four West Coast schools in the second year of its $7 billion deal.

  11. 2014 The College Football Playoff begins with four teams, and the New Year's Six host the semifinals in turn.

    Two teams were not enough, so the formula gave way to a committee: the College Football Playoff, four teams through 2023, with the New Year's Six hosting two semifinals a year in three fixed pairs. ESPN paid about $470 million a year for the first twelve seasons. Alabama, Clemson, Georgia and Ohio State filled 22 of the 40 places. The 2014 bracket and the first rotation are drawn on the other tab.

  12. 2018 The transfer portal opens on 15 October.

    The NCAA transfer portal let coaches call a player the day his name appeared, where the old school had been able to block them. The season on the bench remained until 2021. The 2018 rules are drawn on the other tab.

  13. 2021 Alston on 21 June, name, image and likeness on 1 July, one free transfer in April: the busiest year of the law.

    The Supreme Court said what the Ninth Circuit had said, unanimously, six years later. NCAA v. Alston covered only education-linked pay, but its reasoning left the NCAA no antitrust shelter for the rest of its pay rules. Justice Kavanaugh wrote separately that the rest of those rules "raise serious questions" and ended with "The NCAA is not above the law." Nine days later the NCAA gave up a larger fight and let athletes earn money from their name, image and likeness; Florida's state law took effect on 1 July 2021 and several states followed. On 15 April a first transfer had already been allowed to play at once.

    Before the ruling the education rung is a red dashed stub: computers, instruments, tutoring, study abroad, graduate scholarships and paid internships are each capped or banned by NCAA rule. On 21 June 2021 the nine justices voted 9-0 that the NCAA may not cap them, and the rung runs off the right edge: no cap at all. The struck $5,000 box returns as a wider one: schools may now pay up to $5,980 a year in academic awards. The 2021 state of the money-flow figure, on the other tab, shows the outside arrows going live.

  14. 2022 to 2024 USC and UCLA leave for the Big Ten; the Pac-12 collapses to two schools; thirteen move in one summer.

    USC and UCLA announced for the Big Ten on 30 June 2022. The Pac-12's best offer to the other ten schools was a streaming deal worth about $20 to 25 million a school, and all ten left within fourteen months: Oregon and Washington to the Big Ten, Colorado, Arizona, Arizona State and Utah to the Big 12, Cal and Stanford to the ACC, with SMU. Texas and Oklahoma joined the SEC the same summer. Oregon State and Washington State were left, and the Power Five became the Power Four. The 2024 state of the Pac-12 map and the 2024 arrows show where everyone went.

  15. 2024 Twelve teams: five champions, seven at-large, four byes, and Ohio State wins as an 8 seed.

    The five highest-ranked conference champions got in automatically and the four best of those took the byes, so No. 9 Boise State and No. 12 Arizona State rested while higher-ranked teams played a first round on campus. Ohio State won it all, beating Notre Dame 34-23 on 20 January 2025. The management committee changed the rule for 2025 and seeded straight down the rankings; Indiana won that bracket as the 1 seed. The 2024 bracket is on the other tab.

  16. 2025 The House settlement: $2.8 billion in back pay, a capped payroll, a clearinghouse.

    Grant House, an Arizona State swimmer, and Sedona Prince, a basketball player, sued in June 2020 over the NIL and broadcast money athletes had been denied. The NCAA and the five power conferences agreed to settle on 23 May 2024, and Judge Wilken, the same judge as in O'Bannon, approved the House settlement on 6 June 2025. From 1 July 2025 schools may pay athletes directly under Revenue sharing, and the College Sports Commission polices the cap and the outside deals.

    The first slab is paid in 2025-26, about $280 million; by 2029-30 half the fund stands, and at 2034-35 the paid total reads $2.8 billion. Every slab is cut the same way: the NCAA's reserves pay about $1.1 billion of it, the five power conferences about $664 million, and the other 27 Division I conferences, who never saw the football money, about $990 million. It goes to athletes who played from 2016 to 2024.

  17. 2026 The Pac-12 relaunches with eight; the playoff stays at twelve; the Big Ten and SEC deadlock over 16 or 24.

    The Pac-12 relaunched on 1 July 2026 with the two survivors, five schools from the Mountain West and Texas State. The playoff's format for 2026 stayed at twelve when the Big Ten and SEC missed the 23 January deadline, with three new rules: every Power Four champion in, the best Group of Six team in, Notre Dame in if ranked in the top twelve. The management committee discussed a 24-team model on 30 September 2026 without a vote and has until 1 December 2026 to change 2027. The 16-team and 24-team brackets are on the other tab.

Final words

Television money sorts the schools into conferences. The two richest conferences set the playoff's shape and take 58% of its money. The players, after three antitrust cases, are paid from the same money through a capped payroll and a clearinghouse. Each piece is a contract or a court order laid over the last one. The next piece, a 24-team bracket or a federal law, is due for a decision by 1 December 2026.

Glossary
Conference ↩
A league of schools, usually eight to eighteen, that play one another every season, crown a champion, and act as one business. The league sells its members' television rights and splits the money, and a school that belongs to no league is an independent. source
Football Bowl Subdivision ↩
The top level of college football, with 138 schools in 2026, whose conferences and independents run their own postseason. It is the only level of college football whose champion is not decided by a tournament that the national governing body runs. source
Media rights deal ↩
A conference's multi-year contract that sells its football and basketball broadcast rights to television networks and streaming services. It is the largest source of money a conference shares with its members, and the biggest one runs about $7 billion over 2023-30. source
NCAA v. Board of Regents of the University of Oklahoma ↩
The 1984 Supreme Court decision that the national governing body's plan to control college football television broadcasts violated antitrust law. It freed schools and conferences to sell their own television rights, which made every later Media rights deal possible. source
Conference realignment ↩
The recurring reshuffling of which schools belong to which conference, driven mainly by the size of each conference's Media rights deal. The latest wave ran from 2021 to 2026 and left the Pac-12 with two members before it was rebuilt. source
Southeastern Conference ↩
A 16-school league based in Birmingham, Alabama, that added Texas and Oklahoma in 2024 and is one of the four richest conferences. Its exclusive Media rights deal with ESPN began in 2024, and its revenue was about $1.1 billion in 2024-25. source
Big Ten Conference ↩
An 18-school league based in Rosemont, Illinois, that added USC, UCLA, Oregon and Washington from the West Coast in 2024. Its roughly $7 billion Media rights deal with Fox, CBS and NBC for 2023-30 makes it the top payer, about $80 million per full-share school. source
Atlantic Coast Conference ↩
A league with 17 football schools that added Cal, Stanford and SMU in 2024 and counts Notre Dame as a member in other sports. Its ESPN Media rights deal runs to 2036, and its roughly $47 million per-school payout trails the Southeastern Conference and Big Ten Conference. source
Big 12 Conference ↩
A 16-school league based in Irving, Texas, formed in 1994 from the Big Eight and four Texas schools, with play from 1996. It lost Texas and Oklahoma to the Southeastern Conference in 2024 but added eight schools in 2023 and 2024, including BYU, Arizona and Utah. source
Pac-12 Conference ↩
The West Coast league, founded in 1915, that lost ten of twelve members in 2024 and relaunched on July 1, 2026 with eight football schools. It now ranks with the second-tier leagues, under Media rights deals with CBS and The CW that run through 2030-31. source
Power Four ↩
The four richest conferences in the Football Bowl Subdivision, the Atlantic Coast Conference, Big Ten Conference, Big 12 Conference and Southeastern Conference. They write their own athlete-benefit rules, and the group shrank from five to four when the Pac-12 Conference fell to two members in 2024. source
Group of Five ↩
The Football Bowl Subdivision conferences outside the Power Four, such as the Sun Belt and the Mountain West. The rebuilt Pac-12 Conference now counts as a sixth member, so the group is also called the Group of Six. source
FBS independent ↩
A Football Bowl Subdivision program that belongs to no conference for football, so it builds its own schedule and signs its own Media rights deal. Only Notre Dame and UConn remain in 2026, after BYU, Army and UMass joined conferences. source
Bowl game ↩
A one-off postseason college football game that a local civic or commercial committee runs and that invites two teams after the season ends. The name comes from the bowl-shaped Rose Bowl stadium in Pasadena, and for most of history bowls were exhibitions with no championship attached. source
AP Poll ↩
The weekly Top 25 ranking that the Associated Press has compiled since 1936 from the votes of about 62 sportswriters and broadcasters. Its final poll, taken after the bowl games since 1968, names the AP national champion. source
Bowl Coalition ↩
The 1992-1994 agreement among five conferences, Notre Dame and seven bowls to send the two highest-ranked teams to one bowl game as a title game. The Big Ten Conference and Pac-12 Conference stayed out because their champions were locked into the Rose Bowl. source
Bowl Championship Series ↩
The 1998-2013 system that used human polls and computer formulas to pair the top two teams in one title game. It grew out of the Bowl Coalition and finally brought the Big Ten Conference, the Pac-12 Conference and the Rose Bowl into the system. source
College Football Playoff ↩
The single-elimination tournament that has decided the Football Bowl Subdivision national champion since the 2014 season, replacing the Bowl Championship Series. A 13-member committee picks and orders the field, which had four teams through 2023 and twelve from 2024. source
New Year's Six ↩
The six bowl games under contract to the College Football Playoff, namely the Rose, Sugar, Orange, Cotton, Fiesta and Peach. In the four-team era two of them hosted the semifinals each year, and in the twelve-team bracket all six host playoff games. source
16-team format ↩
A College Football Playoff bracket with sixteen teams, the expansion size the Southeastern Conference favored for 2026 and later. The Big Ten Conference pushed for 24 teams instead, and the deadlock kept the twelve-team bracket for 2026. source
Amateurism ↩
The principle that college athletes compete for an education and may not be paid to play. Courts narrowed it in a series of antitrust cases, and since July 2025 schools may pay athletes directly. source
O'Bannon v. NCAA ↩
The 2009 antitrust lawsuit by former UCLA basketball player Ed O'Bannon over unpaid use of college athletes' likenesses in video games and broadcasts. A 2014 ruling let schools pay up to full cost of attendance plus $5,000 a year; a 2015 appeal cut it to cost of attendance. source
NCAA v. Alston ↩
The unanimous 2021 Supreme Court decision that caps on education-linked benefits for college athletes, such as paid internships, violate antitrust law. It covered only education-linked pay, but it removed Amateurism as a legal shield against paying players at all. source
Name, image and likeness ↩
The commercial value of an athlete's identity, which outside parties pay for through endorsements, appearances, social media posts and autographs. College athletes could not earn it until July 1, 2021, and it is distinct from money the school itself pays. source
NCAA transfer portal ↩
An online database, launched October 15, 2018, that lists athletes who have told their school they intend to transfer. Once an athlete is listed, coaches at other schools may contact them, which makes transferring a yearly open market. source
House settlement ↩
The court-approved agreement, effective July 1, 2025, that settled three antitrust lawsuits by college athletes over pay. It pays about $2.8 billion to past athletes and lets schools pay current athletes directly, starting at about $20.5 million per school a year. source
Revenue sharing ↩
Direct payment of athletes from a school's own athletic budget, which the House settlement allowed from July 1, 2025. Unlike Name, image and likeness money from outside parties, it comes from the school, up to $20.5 million per school in 2025-26. source
College Sports Commission ↩
The enforcement body, led by CEO Bryan Seeley, that the power conferences launched on June 6, 2025 to police House settlement rules. It polices the yearly cap on Revenue sharing, enforces roster caps, and reviews outside Name, image and likeness deals. source
