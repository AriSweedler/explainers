The Hebrew Calendar

Twelve moons fall eleven days short of a year, and no whole number of days fills either. Every calendar is a patch over that arithmetic, and the Hebrew calendar carries the most visible patches: a thirteenth month in seven years out of nineteen, and two months that grow or shrink by a day. This article builds the calendar one part at a time, from the day to the year. It passes through the solar calendars, which patch the year with a day, and the nineteen-year cycle, which patches it with a month. It ends at the rules that fix the length of every Hebrew year.

Cycles and solar calendars

Earth turns once against the Sun from one noon to the next, and that turn is a Solar day: about 24 hours, on average 86,400 seconds. Nothing in the sky divides evenly into it.

The red dot on Earth's rim is a town. It starts at noon, and the dashed red line through it points at the Sun. The dotted yellow line points at a distant star and never moves. The orange arrow to the Sun creeps about one degree a day as Earth moves along its orbit. After 23 hours 56 minutes the town points at the star again, but not yet at the Sun: the red arc between the star line and the Sun line is the turn the town still owes, and the inset magnifies it. That arc closes at 24 hours, 86,400 seconds.

Earth moves along its orbit while it spins, so a noon-to-noon turn is a little more than one full rotation. Calendars count noons, not star-risings, because daily life follows the Sun; that is why the day is 24 hours and not 23 hours 56 minutes.

From one new moon to the next, when the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun and disappears, is a Synodic month: 29.530589 Solar days, or 29 days 12 hours 44 minutes.

The grey Moon circles Earth with its lit half toward the Sun. It is new each time it crosses the orange Sun line. The lit part of the small disc is the Moon as seen from Earth. The grey bar along the bottom counts the days: one month at 29.53, the twelfth new moon at day 354.37, the thirteenth at 383.90. The year falls between them.

One full cycle of the seasons, spring to spring, is a Tropical year: 365.24219 Solar days, or 365 days and almost 6 hours.

Earth starts on the red spring mark, the spring equinox, the moment spring begins, and leaves a blue trail as it goes round. On the ruler the blue bar counts whole days. At day 365 it stops at the red sliver that 365 days leave uncovered, 5 hours 49 minutes. Only at 365.24219 days does it reach spring again.

Twelve Synodic months stop 10.88 days short of that mark and thirteen overshoot it by 18.66. A calendar has to choose which error to live with, or trade one against the other. Solar calendars ignore the Moon and patch the year with a day.

A calendar of 365 whole days is 5.8 hours short of the Tropical year. The shortfall compounds: one whole day every 4.13 years, and spring arrives a day later on the calendar each time. The repair is a Leap day, one extra day that turns a 365-day year into a 366-day one.

The seven cells are 18 to 24 March, and the red line is the moment of the spring equinox on a 365-day calendar. From the dashed yellow line, where spring fell in year 0, the red band grows 5.8 hours a year. At year 4 it is nearly a cell wide, and at 4.13 years spring has slipped a whole day. Switch the Leap day on with the button under the figure and by year 8 two added days have pulled spring back, an hour and a half early instead of two days late; the dashed red line shows where it would have fallen without them. The only question is how often to add a Leap day.

Julius Caesar's answer, in force from 45 BC, was every fourth year without exception: the Julian calendar. Its average year is 365.25 days, 11 minutes 15 seconds longer than the Tropical year.

The dotted yellow cell is 21 March, the date the Easter tables, the church's tables for dating Easter drawn up in 325, reserved for the equinox and never moved. The red cell is the day the equinox falls on, with the red dot inside it sliding one cell to the left every 128 years. The blue marker and the blue bar on the ruler count the years, and each red tick under the ruler is one more day of drift: one by 453, five by 965, and by 1582 the equinox falls on 11 March, ten days early.

Pope Gregory XIII's reform of 1582 deleted ten dates and changed one rule: a century year, one ending in 00, skips its Leap day unless it is divisible by 400. That is the Gregorian calendar, with 97 Leap days in every 400 years.

Under the old rule every fourth year is a blue cell with 366 days: 100 in the block. Switch the century rule on with the button under the figure: 1700, 1800 and 1900 lose their Leap day, while 2000, divisible by 400, keeps its 366-day year: 97 Leap days in 400 years. The average year becomes

Leap months and the molad

Lay whole Tropical years and whole Synodic months side by side and look for a place where both end together. The first good match is the Metonic cycle: 19 years, 6939.60 days, against 235 months, 6939.69 days.

The orange rail counts Tropical years and the grey rail counts Synodic months. The lower panel magnifies the dashed slit at the end of the years: the orange tick marks the year's end, the nearest new moon sits beside it, and the red gap between them is the miss. At 8 years the miss is 1.6 days and at 11 years 1.5 days; at 19 years the gap arrow vanishes and the new moon sits on the tick.

The two rails miss by 0.087 day, about 2 hours 5 minutes, after 19 years. Since 235 = 19 × 12 + 7, a calendar that follows the cycle gives twelve years 12 months and seven years 13.

The thirteenth month is a Leap month: a whole extra month that turns a 12-month year of about 354 days into a 13-month year of about 384. A 12-month year falls 10.88 days behind the seasons; a 13-month year runs 18.66 days ahead.

Each grey cell is a 12-month year and each blue cell a 13-month year. The grey trace plots where each year begins against the spring equinox line, and the red ring marks the end of the cycle. With no extra months the trace sinks 206.6 days; at six it ends 29 days low, at eight 30 days high, and at seven it comes back to the line.

Twelve shortfalls total 130.5 days and seven overshoots 130.6, so exactly seven Leap months in nineteen years bring the year-starts back to where they began, two hours high. Which seven is a choice.

The Hebrew calendar is the calendar of Judaism. It places its Leap months in years 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17 and 19 of each Metonic cycle. For now we ignore how the calendar chooses the first day of each year; that is the last section.

Every cycle closes about two hours late: 2 hours 7 minutes with the month length the calendar actually uses, defined below. The mean year is therefore 365.2468 days, 6.7 minutes longer than the Tropical year. Dates slip one day later against the seasons every 216 years. That is steeper than the Gregorian calendar's day in 3,200 years and gentler than the Julian calendar's day in 128.

The calendar does not watch the sky for the new moon; it computes it. Each computed new moon is the previous one plus a fixed Molad interval of 29 days, 12 hours and 793 parts, at 1080 parts to the hour: 29.530594 days, or 29 d 12 h 44 min 3⅓ s.

The 24-hour dial shows one day. The grey hand, held at the top, is the real new moon at its mean rate. The red hand is the computed one; it drifts clockwise by 0.44 seconds a month, 5 seconds a year. The red arc between them is the share of a day the computed new moon runs late. A new moon a tenth of a day late crosses into the next day in a tenth of years, so the arc is also the share of years the computed new moon lands on the wrong day. After one year the hands still overlap; after 656 years the arc is one hour; at today's 97 minutes it covers 6.7% of the dial, and at 3,000 years it passes four and a half hours.

The interval exceeds the Synodic month of 29.530589 days by 0.44 seconds. The computed new moon of the first month fixes the year's first day.

Shaping the Hebrew year

The year begins on the day of the computed new moon of the first month, unless one of the Rosh Hashanah postponement rules moves it. Hours count from 6 PM, the nominal sunset, so 18 hours is noon.

The grey line drops from the marker into the day of the computed new moon, with its hours and parts read out above. The red block below is the day the year begins, and the red arrow appears whenever a rule moves it. The block only rests on a solid blue stretch of the dashed rail: Monday, Tuesday, Thursday or Saturday. The readout counts the days postponed with their share of years, and the rule list lights the rule that fired.

In a quiet week nothing moves. Four rules move the first day:

  1. A new moon on a Sunday, Wednesday or Friday: the first day steps to the next day.
  2. A new moon at or after noon: the first day steps one day, and one more if that day is forbidden.
  3. A Tuesday new moon at or after 9 hours 204 parts in a 12-month year, inside the yellow sliver: to Thursday, two days postponed.
  4. A Monday new moon at or after 15 hours 589 parts in the year after a 13-month year: to Tuesday.

The last two exist only to keep the year's length in range. The first day falls on the new moon's own day in 39% of years, one day later in 47%, two days later in 14%.

Whatever the postponement, the year's length is the gap to the next first day: 353, 354 or 355 days, or 383, 384 or 385 with a Leap month. Two months absorb the difference.

Every month has a fixed length except two. Cheshvan, the second month of the year, normally has 29 days and takes a 30th when the Rosh Hashanah postponement rules leave the year at 355 or 385 days.

The first grey bar is the first month, always 30 days. The red bar is Cheshvan and the blue bar is Kislev, the month after it; the 29-day and 30-day lines sit behind them. Kislev, the month after Cheshvan, normally has 30 days and drops to 29 when the year needs 353 or 383. It can only lose, and it never loses while Cheshvan gains, so the pair reads 29 + 29, 29 + 30 or 30 + 30 and never 30 + 29.

No cycle divides the Solar day. A Leap month tops up the twelve Synodic months seven times in nineteen years. Two months swallow the last day or two. That is the whole calendar.

Final words

A day, a month and a year that refuse to divide one another, and a fix that holds to two hours in nineteen years, worked out by hand more than a thousand years ago. The Hebrew calendar is what it looks like when a people decide to count the Moon and keep the seasons anyway.

Glossary
Solar day ↩
The time for Earth to turn once relative to the Sun, from one noon to the next: about 24 hours. Its yearly average of 86,400 seconds is the day that every calendar counts.
Synodic month ↩
The mean time from one new moon to the next: 29.530589 days, about 29 days 12 hours 44 minutes. Twelve of them total 354.37 days, and thirteen total 383.90 days.
Tropical year ↩
The time for one full cycle of the seasons: 365.24219 days, or 365 days and almost 6 hours. Twelve Synodic months fall about 11 days short of it, and thirteen run about 19 days long.
Leap day ↩
One extra day that turns a 365-day year into a 366-day year. A 365-day year is 5.8 hours shorter than a Tropical year, so it drifts one day against the seasons every 4.13 years.
Julian calendar ↩
The calendar of Julius Caesar, in use from 45 BC: 365-day years, with a Leap day every fourth year without exception. Its 365.25-day average year is 11 minutes longer than the Tropical year, so it drifts one day every 128 years.
Gregorian calendar ↩
The 1582 reform of the Julian calendar: century years such as 1900 skip the Leap day unless divisible by 400. Its average year of 365.2425 days is about 27 seconds longer than the Tropical year: one day of drift in 3,200 years.
Metonic cycle ↩
The near match between 19 Tropical years and 235 Synodic months: 6939.60 days against 6939.69 days, a gap of about 2 hours. Since 235 is 19 times 12 plus 7, a calendar built on it adds a thirteenth month in 7 of every 19 years.
Leap month ↩
A whole thirteenth month that turns a 12-month year of about 354 days into a 13-month year of about 384 days. Seven of them per Metonic cycle cancel the 11-day yearly shortfall of 12 Synodic months against the Tropical year.
Hebrew calendar ↩
The calendar of Judaism, which adds a Leap month in years 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17 and 19 of each Metonic cycle. Its mean year of 365.2468 days is 6.7 minutes longer than the Tropical year, so it drifts one day every 216 years.
Molad interval ↩
The fixed time the Hebrew calendar puts between computed new moons: 29 days, 12 hours and 793 parts, at 1080 parts per hour. That equals 29.530594 days, about half a second longer than the Synodic month of 29.530589 days.
Rosh Hashanah postponement rules ↩
Four rules that push the first day of the Hebrew calendar year up to two days past its computed new moon. The result is a year of 353, 354 or 355 days, or 383, 384 or 385 days with a Leap month.
Cheshvan ↩
The second month of the Hebrew calendar year, in October or November, with 29 or 30 days. It takes 30 days when the Rosh Hashanah postponement rules give the year 355 or 385 days.
Kislev ↩
The month of the Hebrew calendar after Cheshvan, in November or December, with 30 or 29 days. It drops to 29 days when the Rosh Hashanah postponement rules give the year 353 or 383 days.
