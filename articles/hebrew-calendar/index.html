The Hebrew Calendar

Twelve moons fall eleven days short of a year, and no whole number of days fills either. Every calendar is a patch over that arithmetic. The Hebrew calendar carries the most visible patches: a thirteenth month in seven years out of nineteen, and two months that grow or shrink by a day. The day is the unit. The solar calendars patch the year with a day, and the nineteen-year cycle patches it with a month. Four rules then fix the length of every Hebrew year.

Three cycles and two patches

Three cycles set every calendar: Earth's turn, the Moon's orbit and the seasons. The shortest is the day. Earth turns once against the Sun from one noon to the next, and that turn is a Solar day: about 24 hours, on average 86,400 seconds. It is the unit every calendar counts. Neither the month nor the year is a whole number of them, and everything that follows comes from that.

The red dot on Earth's rim is a town, starting at noon, and the dashed red line through it shows where its noon points. The dotted yellow line points at a distant star and never moves; the orange arrow to the Sun creeps about 1° a day as Earth moves along its orbit. After 23 hours 56 minutes the town points at the star again, but not yet at the Sun. The red arc between the star line and the Sun line is the turn the town still owes, and the inset magnifies it so the 1° is visible. The arc closes at 24 hours, 86,400 seconds. There the readout for turns against the Sun reads 1.0000 and the one for turns against the stars a little more.

Noon to noon is a little more than one full turn, because Earth moves along its orbit while it spins: 24 hours, not 23 hours 56 minutes. Calendars count noons, since daily life follows the Sun. The 86,400 seconds are a yearly average: a single noon-to-noon day runs a little longer or shorter, and the figure draws the mean. Every calendar count is a count of these days.

The Moon shows a different shape every night. Only the half that faces the Sun is lit, and how much of that half faces us changes as the Moon moves round Earth. Once each orbit the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun, and its dark side faces us: a New moon. The instant itself cannot be seen. A calendar of the Moon starts its month there, or at the thin crescent one or two Solar days later, the first sign that the New moon has passed. From one New moon to the next is a Synodic month: 29.530589 Solar days, or 29 days 12 hours 44 minutes.

The grey Moon circles Earth with its lit half always turned toward the Sun, and every crossing of the orange arrow to the Sun is a New moon. The large disc at top right is the Moon as seen from Earth. At day 0 its lit area is nothing: the disc is dark. By day 1.97, 24° round the orbit, 4% of the disc is lit, a thin crescent on the right, the first thing anyone sees. The grey bar along the bottom ruler counts the days: one month at 29.53. A tick marks the twelfth New moon at day 354.37, and another tick the thirteenth at day 383.90.

The figure draws the mean. Single months run from 29.27 to 29.83 days, so a calendar of the Moon alternates 29-day and 30-day months and holds the mean over the year. A calendar that waits for the crescent starts its month a day or two after one that takes the instant; two calendars of the Moon can differ by a day. Two New moons bound a length, and the length is what a calendar counts: twelve of them, or thirteen. The year ends somewhere between the twelfth tick and the thirteenth. To say where takes a mark in the seasons as sharp as the New moon is in the sky.

The seasons give that mark. Each March the Sun crosses northward over Earth's equator, and northern spring begins at that instant: the Vernal equinox, which falls on 19, 20 or 21 March. Calendars that follow the seasons measure their year from it. One full cycle of the seasons, spring to spring, is a Tropical year: 365.24219 Solar days, or 365 days 5 hours 48 minutes 45 seconds. No whole number of days closes it, and no whole number of Synodic months does either. The figure runs both counts against one spring.

The dashed red line through both lanes at day 365.24 is spring again, and the ruler beneath magnifies the dashed red slit on the chosen lane. On the Moon lane the red band after the twelfth cell is 10.88 days, and the readout says thirteen months would end 18.66 days past spring.

Both lanes end early. A year with no extra unit is a Common year: 365 days when the calendar follows the Sun, twelve Synodic months when it follows the Moon. The Sun's count misses spring by 0.24 day; the Moon's misses it by 10.88 days, and thirteen months overshoot by 18.66. No way of counting removes either gap, because neither is a whole day or a whole month. A calendar can add a whole unit now and then. A quarter of a day owed each year makes a whole day in about four years; eleven days owed each year make a whole month every second or third year.

Take the Sun's gap first, the smaller one. A 365-day Common year ends 5.8 hours before the Tropical year does, so spring arrives 5.8 hours later on the calendar each year. The shortfall compounds to one whole day every 4.13 years. The repair is a Leap day, one extra day that stretches a 365-day Common year to 366 days and pulls spring back a whole day. Western calendars add it as 29 February.

The seven day-cells are 18 to 24 March, and the red line is the moment of the Vernal equinox on the calendar. From the dashed yellow line, where spring fell in year 0 at the start of 21 March, the red band grows 5.8 hours a year. At year 4 it is 23 hours 15 minutes wide, nearly a whole cell, and at 4.13 years spring has slipped one whole day, to 22 March. With the Leap day switched on, by year 8 two added days have pulled spring back: 1 hour 30 minutes early instead of two days late. The dashed red line shows where spring would fall without them.

This figure is the whole patch pattern: the calendar keeps a bare count, lets the error grow, and repays it with one whole unit once a whole unit is owed. A year that receives the extra unit, a day or a month, is a Leap year, one unit longer than a Common year: 366 days, or 13 months. The calendar needs a rule because the owed day arrives at 4.13 years, not 4: any rule repays whole days for a fractional debt, and the difference accumulates. The rule here repays 24 hours for every 23 hours 15 minutes owed, so spring creeps 45 minutes early every four years. Each calendar's rule for which years get the extra unit is what sets its drift.

The rule in that figure is Julius Caesar's, in force from 45 BCE (Before the Common Era, the same count as BC). Every fourth year is a Leap year, without exception. That rule is the Julian calendar, and its mean year is 365.25 days, 11 minutes 15 seconds longer than the Tropical year. Forty-five minutes every four years is small; the figure lets it run from 325 to 1582 on one page of the calendar.

The dotted yellow cell is 21 March, the date the church's Easter reckoning fixed for the Vernal equinox and never moved; in 325 the two coincide. The red-outlined cell is the day of March the Vernal equinox falls on, and the red dot inside it slides one cell to the left every 128 years. The blue marker and the blue bar on the century ruler count the years since 325, and each red tick under the ruler is one more day of drift. After 128 years the Vernal equinox has crept one whole day earlier, to 20 March; by 1582 spring arrives on 11 March, ten days before the date the calendar says.

Eleven minutes a year is the price of a rule with no exceptions. The surplus reaches a whole day every 128 years, 3.1 days in 400. The figure places the Vernal equinox on 21 March in 325 to match the church's date; the astronomical equinox of that year fell nearer 20 March. The Julian calendar over-repays. A calendar that skips three of its Leap days in every 400 years takes back three of those 3.1 days.

That is what Pope Gregory XIII's reform of 1582 did. It deleted ten dates, so that Thursday 4 October was followed by Friday 15 October. It also changed one rule: a century year, one ending in 00, skips its Leap day unless it is divisible by 400. The calendar with that rule is the Gregorian calendar, with 97 Leap days in every 400 years instead of 100.

Under the old rule every fourth year is a blue cell with 366 days: 100 in the block of 400. Switch the century rule on, and 1700, 1800 and 1900 take a red outline: the century rule skips their Leap day. 2000, divisible by 400, takes a yellow outline and keeps its 366-day year. The count reads 97 in 400 years. The days in 400 years fall from 146,100 to 146,097, and the drift readout drops to one day in about 3,200 years. The mean year becomes

The rule runs on arithmetic alone: the last skipped Leap day was 1900's, and the next will be 2100's. The day patch is finished: a rule, one exception to it, and a residual of one day in 3,200 years.

The Moon has had none of this. Twelve Synodic months still fall 10.88 days short of the Tropical year, the gap the Tropical year figure's Moon lane showed. The Gregorian calendar does not count them at all.

A calendar whose months follow the Synodic month and whose years follow the Tropical year, a Lunisolar calendar, patches with a whole month now and then. The month it inserts is a Leap month, the year's thirteenth. It turns a 12-month year of about 354 days into a 13-month Leap year of about 384. The year's length therefore swings by 30 days, where the year of a calendar of the Sun swings by one. Each 12-month year begins earlier in the seasons than the last and each 13-month year later, by the two gaps the Tropical year's Moon lane showed. The count runs over nineteen years; for now take the span as given.

Each outlined grey cell is a 12-month year and each solid blue cell a 13-month year. The grey trace plots where each year's first day falls against the dashed orange line, the Vernal equinox. The red ring at year 19 marks where the cycle ends relative to where it started. Twelve shortfalls of 10.875 days total 130.50 days and seven overshoots of 18.655 total 130.59; the 0.09 day between them is the miss the next figure measures. So exactly seven Leap months in nineteen years bring the year-starts back to where they began. A calendar of the Sun spends its Leap day about one year in four; a Lunisolar calendar spends its Leap month seven years in nineteen.

Every month here is the mean Synodic month, and every year starts at an exact multiple of it. A real first day can sit a day or two off that mark, for reasons the second section adds. Why nineteen?

The first close match is the Metonic cycle: 19 years, 6939.60 days, against 235 months, 6939.69 days.

The two rails miss by 0.087 day after nineteen years, about 2 hours 5 minutes, so New moons return to the same dates of the seasons every nineteen years. Meton of Athens introduced the cycle in 432 BCE and rounded it to 6,940 days. Nineteen years of twelve months hold 228 months, and

235=19×12+7235 = 19 \times 12 + 7235=19×12+7

so a calendar that follows the cycle gives twelve of its years 12 months and seven of them a Leap month. That is the seven the slider found, and the two hours are the gap the red ring at year 19 of the opening figure marks. Nineteen years is not a perfect match either: those two hours are the residual every calendar built on the cycle inherits. Which seven of the nineteen years get one is a choice, and each calendar makes its own.

The Hebrew calendar, the Lunisolar calendar of Judaism, has 30-day and 29-day months and follows the Metonic cycle. Its seven Leap months fall in years 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17 and 19 of each cycle, the spread the slider drew at seven. Its year begins in autumn.

The calendar places the seven for one festival. Passover, the spring festival that begins on day 15 of the spring month, lasts seven days in Israel and traditionally eight elsewhere. It must fall in spring, and the calendar adds its Leap month so that day 15 never slips before the Vernal equinox. Passover falls a fixed 163 days before the next year's first day, so the figure can follow either one.

The cycle does not close: each cycle of the Hebrew calendar ends about two hours late, so its dates slip roughly a day later against the seasons in two centuries. That slope is steeper than the Gregorian calendar's and gentler than the Julian calendar's. No rule repairs it. Where the Gregorian calendar skips a Leap day to cancel the Julian calendar's excess, the Hebrew calendar lets the residual stand; the 7.7 days since 359 follow.

The exact rate depends on the month length the calendar computes with, which the next section gives. The cycle has answered which years get the Leap month. It leaves open where each year's first day lands, in autumn, and how long the year runs to the next.

The Hebrew year

Three facts fix a Hebrew calendar year: the date of its first day, whether the Metonic cycle gives it a Leap month, and the date of the next first day. The cycle has given the second. The first comes next. For now every month keeps its standard length; the third fact, and with it the year's length, comes at the end of this section.

The count of years advances on that first day. Anno Mundi, the Hebrew calendar's year count from autumn 3761 BCE, abbreviated AM, is the CE year plus 3760, or plus 3761 once the autumn new year has passed. AM 5786 began on 23 September 2025.

That day begins Tishrei, the 30-day month that opens the year in September or October. From the new year, Tishrei is month 1; from Passover's month, the spring month, it is the seventh. Its first day, 1 Tishrei, is Rosh Hashanah, the Hebrew new year and the anchor from which the Hebrew calendar computes every date. It falls between 5 September and 5 October.

The red arrow points down at 1 Tishrei, and the grey block above it, 5 September to 5 October, is the only range it can fall in. The outlined strip hanging from the arrow is the whole year at standard month lengths, 354 days. It slides as one piece, and its blue first cell is Tishrei. The red tick under the strip is Passover, 191 days after 1 Tishrei; the grey axis below gives the Gregorian dates.

At 23 September 2025, the real Rosh Hashanah of AM 5786, the Passover readout gives 2 April 2026, the real date. With the Leap month in, a 30-day olive cell joins the strip and pushes Passover to 221 days after 1 Tishrei. The earliest 1 Tishrei, 5 September, needs a Leap month and puts Passover on 14 April; the latest, 5 October, does the same with 12 months.

Both ends of the range give Passover on 14 April because the Leap month's 30 days and the 30-day range cancel. A year that starts as early as 5 September must hold a Leap month; without it Passover would fall on 15 March, before the Vernal equinox.

The strip is a simplification: every month at its standard length, 354 or 384 days. A real year can run a day longer or shorter, and the date of the next 1 Tishrei decides which.

The Hebrew calendar does not watch the sky for the New moon; it computes one. The Molad is the instant of mean New moon the calendar computes for each month, written as a weekday and an hour. The calendar's day runs from sunset to sunset, taken as 6 PM. A Molad's weekday is therefore the day that holds it by that clock. Hour 18 is noon.

Only one Molad enters the computation, the one for Tishrei. For AM 5786 it fell on Monday 22 September 2025, 18.2 hours after Sunday's 6 PM, ten minutes past noon. For now take the year to begin on the Molad's own day; a later figure adds the four rules that can move it one or two days.

Twelve Molads carry the calendar from one Tishrei to the next, thirteen in a year with a Leap month; only the last, the Molad of Tishrei, sets the year's start. The true New moon can sit on a different calendar day; nothing in the calendar depends on it. The dots are evenly spaced because each Molad is the previous one plus one fixed step, written in days, hours and a unit smaller than the minute.

That unit is the Part, 1/1080 of an hour, or 3⅓ seconds, so an hour holds 1,080 and a day 25,920. 1,080 divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9 and 10.

That step is the Molad interval, the fixed month length between one Molad and the next: 29 days, 12 hours and 793 Parts. That is 29.530594 days. Every Molad is therefore a whole count of days, hours and Parts; no fraction enters the arithmetic. The interval exceeds the mean Synodic month, 29.530589 days, by 0.44 seconds. Each computed Molad falls a little later than the mean New moon, and the lag accumulates.

The Sun is the bigger miss. 235 intervals overrun 19 Tropical years by 2 hours 7 minutes, two minutes more than the 2 hours 5 minutes the Metonic cycle leaves with the true Synodic month. The calendar's mean year is therefore 365.2468 days, 6.7 minutes over the Tropical year: one day every 216 years. That is the exact rate the Hebrew calendar figure deferred.

Twelve intervals make 354 days 8 hours 876 Parts, 50 weeks plus 4 days 8 hours 876 Parts. Thirteen make 383 days 21 hours 589 Parts, 54 weeks plus 5 days 21 hours 589 Parts. The remainder past the whole weeks is where the next Molad of Tishrei lands in the week, and its day is the candidate for the next Rosh Hashanah. The calendar does not always take it.

The year does not always begin on the Molad's own day. Four rules decide where it begins: the Rosh Hashanah postponement rules, which push Rosh Hashanah 0, 1 or 2 days past the Molad of Tishrei. Only Monday, Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday survive them: those four are the open days, and Sunday, Wednesday and Friday are closed. The first two patches chase the Sun and the Moon; this one chases the week.

A year runs from one Rosh Hashanah to the next, and both obey the four rules. Each first day moves 0, 1 or 2 days, not always by the same amount for this year and the next. The gap is therefore 353, 354 or 355 whole days, or 383 to 385 with a Leap month. Ten of the twelve months have fixed lengths, 30 and 29 in alternation, 295 days in all; two fill the rest.

Cheshvan, the month after Tishrei, normally has 29 days and takes a 30th when the year needs one more day to reach the next Rosh Hashanah. Kislev, the month after Cheshvan, normally has 30 days and drops to 29 when the year needs one day fewer.

The default is AM 5786. This year's Molad falls on a Monday past noon, so the noon rule pushes the first day to Tuesday. Twelve intervals on, next year's Molad falls on a Saturday, an open day, so next year's first day stays put: 354 days, a Regular year. Now start with a 13-month year and a Wednesday Molad at 2 h. Thirteen intervals carry the next Molad to a Monday past noon, so the rules push both first days one day: 383 days.

Drag to Monday at 4 h. Only Cheshvan grows: the weekday rule pushes the next first day from Friday to Saturday, and the year runs 355 days, a Complete year. Drag to Sunday at 2 h. Only Kislev shrinks: the weekday rule pushes this first day to Monday while the next stays, and the year runs 353 days, a Deficient year. Only Cheshvan can gain and only Kislev can lose. Kislev never loses while Cheshvan gains, so the pair reads 29 + 29, 29 + 30 or 30 + 30, never 30 + 29.

No arrangement of the months reaches 356 or falls to 382. That is why rules 3 and 4 exist: the third keeps a 12-month year under 356, the fourth keeps a year with a Leap month above 382. With a Leap month each shape runs 30 days longer and the pair does not move; the 30 days fill one fixed slot in the spring half of the year.

Adar I is the 30-day Leap month that the Hebrew calendar inserts in cycle years 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17 and 19. It goes in ahead of the 29-day month before Passover's month. That placement leaves Purim and Passover in the months they always hold, so Adar I has no major holiday of its own. That 29-day month carries Purim, the festival on its 14th day, 161 days after 1 Tishrei at standard lengths. Passover follows on the 15th of the month after, 191 days after 1 Tishrei. In a Leap year the displaced 29-day month is Adar II; it keeps Purim on its 14th and is month 13 counted from Passover's month.

Adar I adds 30 days to whichever shape the rules produce and touches neither Cheshvan nor Kislev, so the six year lengths are the three shapes with and without it.

Three shapes, with or without Adar I, on one of four weekdays: 24 combinations, of which 14 occur, seven with 12 months and seven with 13. The rules forbid the rest.

The Molad of Tishrei and the Rosh Hashanah postponement rules set the first day. The gap to the next first day picks the shape: Deficient year, Regular year or Complete year. The Metonic cycle says whether Adar I is in. No cycle divides the Solar day; a Leap month tops up the twelve Synodic months seven times in nineteen years, and two months swallow the last day or two. That is the whole calendar.

Final words

A day, a month and a year that refuse to divide one another. A fix that holds to two hours in nineteen years, worked out by hand more than a thousand years ago. The Hebrew calendar is what it looks like when a people decide to count the Moon and keep the seasons anyway.

Glossary
Solar day ↩
The time for Earth to turn once relative to the Sun, from one local noon to the next: about 24 hours. Its yearly average of 86,400 seconds is the day that every calendar counts.
New moon ↩
The instant the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun, so its unlit side faces Earth and it cannot be seen. Calendars that follow the Moon start each month at it, or at the thin crescent that appears one or two Solar days later.
Synodic month ↩
The mean time from one New moon to the next: 29.530589 Solar days, about 29 days 12 hours 44 minutes. Single months vary from 29.27 to 29.83 days, so calendars alternate 29-day and 30-day months to match the mean.
Vernal equinox ↩
The moment each March when the Sun crosses northward over Earth's equator, which starts spring in the northern hemisphere. It falls on 19, 20 or 21 March, and calendars that follow the seasons measure their year from it.
Tropical year ↩
The time for one full cycle of the seasons: 365.24219 Solar days, close to the interval from one Vernal equinox to the next. Twelve Synodic months fall about 11 days short of it, and thirteen run about 19 days long.
Common year ↩
A calendar year that receives no extra day or month. A calendar that follows the Sun gives it 365 days; one that follows the Moon gives it 12 Synodic months.
Leap day ↩
The extra day that stretches a 365-day Common year to 366 days, because 365 Solar days fall almost six hours short of the Tropical year. Western calendars add it as 29 February; the [Ethiopian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_calendar) adds it as the sixth day of Pagume, its 13th month.
Leap year ↩
A calendar year one unit longer than a Common year: 366 days with a Leap day, or 13 months with an extra month. Each calendar has its own rule for which years get the extra unit, so the calendar keeps pace with the Tropical year.
Julian calendar ↩
The calendar Julius Caesar introduced in 45 BCE: 365-day years plus a Leap day every fourth year without exception, an average of 365.25 days. That runs about 11 minutes longer than the Tropical year, so it gains one day on the seasons every 128 years.
Gregorian calendar ↩
The world's civil calendar: from 1582, the Julian calendar with the Leap day dropped in century years not divisible by 400. Its year averages 365.2425 days, about 27 seconds longer than the Tropical year, one day in roughly 3,200 years.
Lunisolar calendar ↩
A calendar whose months follow the Synodic month and whose years follow the Tropical year. Most years get 12 months and every second or third year gets a 13th, so 7 of every 19 years are Leap years.
Leap month ↩
A whole 13th month that a Lunisolar calendar inserts into a Leap year, which then has about 384 days instead of 354. Seven of them in every 19 years almost cancel the 11-day yearly shortfall against the Tropical year, leaving about 2 hours per cycle.
Metonic cycle ↩
The near-equality of 19 Tropical years, 6939.60 days, and 235 Synodic months, 6939.69 days, a gap of about 2 hours. Because 235 months are 19 years of 12 months plus 7, a Lunisolar calendar adds a Leap month in 7 of every 19 years.
Hebrew calendar ↩
The Lunisolar calendar of Judaism: 30-day and 29-day months, plus a Leap month in 7 of every 19 years, the Metonic cycle. Its year averages 365.2468 days, about 6.7 minutes longer than the Tropical year, so it drifts one day in roughly 216 years.
Passover ↩
The spring festival, seven days in Israel and traditionally eight elsewhere, that begins on day 15 of the Hebrew calendar's spring month. The Hebrew calendar adds its Leap month to keep it in spring, soon after the Vernal equinox, between 26 March and 25 April.
Anno Mundi ↩
The year count of the Hebrew calendar, abbreviated AM, whose year 1 starts at sunset on 6 October 3761 BCE in the Julian calendar. A CE year plus 3760, or plus 3761 after the autumn new year, gives the AM year.
Tishrei ↩
The 30-day month of the Hebrew calendar whose first day is the new year, when the Anno Mundi count advances. It falls in September or October and is the seventh month when you count from the spring month of Passover.
Rosh Hashanah ↩
The Hebrew new year on 1 Tishrei, when the Anno Mundi count advances, and the anchor from which the Hebrew calendar computes every date. It falls between 5 September and 5 October, and every other date of the year is a fixed count of days from it.
Molad ↩
The computed instant of the mean New moon that the Hebrew calendar assigns to each month by arithmetic, not by watching the sky. Only the one for Tishrei enters the computation: it sets the day of Rosh Hashanah, subject to postponements of 0, 1 or 2 days.
Part ↩
The unit of the Hebrew calendar's time arithmetic: 1/1080 of an hour, or 3 1/3 seconds, so an hour holds 1,080 and a day 25,920. Every Molad time is a weekday, a whole number of hours and a whole number of this unit.
Molad interval ↩
The fixed month length the Hebrew calendar assumes between one Molad and the next: 29 days, 12 hours and 793 Parts, or 29.530594 days. It exceeds the mean Synodic month of 29.530589 days by about half a second, so the Molad creeps later than the true New moon.
Rosh Hashanah postponement rules ↩
Four rules that push Rosh Hashanah 0, 1 or 2 days past the Molad of Tishrei, leaving only Monday, Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday. The year's length is the gap to the next first day: 353 to 355 days, or 383 to 385 with a Leap month.
Cheshvan ↩
The month of the Hebrew calendar after Tishrei, in October or November, with no major holidays or fasts. It normally has 29 days but gets a 30th when the year needs one more day to reach the next Rosh Hashanah.
Kislev ↩
The month of the Hebrew calendar after Cheshvan, in November or December, whose 25th day starts [Hanukkah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanukkah). It normally has 30 days but drops to 29 when the year needs one day fewer; only it and Cheshvan vary in length.
Deficient year ↩
A Hebrew calendar year of 353 days, or 383 in a Leap year, in which Cheshvan and Kislev both have 29 days. It is the shortest of the three year shapes that the Rosh Hashanah postponement rules can force.
Regular year ↩
A Hebrew calendar year of 354 days, or 384 in a Leap year, in which Cheshvan has 29 days and Kislev has 30. It is the middle length, one day longer than a Deficient year, with both variable months at their standard lengths.
Complete year ↩
A Hebrew calendar year of 355 days, or 385 in a Leap year, in which Cheshvan and Kislev both have 30 days. It is the longest of the three year shapes, one day longer than a Regular year and two longer than a Deficient year.
Adar I ↩
The 30-day Leap month that the Hebrew calendar inserts in years 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17 and 19 of the Metonic cycle. The regular 29-day month that follows it keeps the usual festivals, so this extra month has no major holiday.
Adar II ↩
The regular 29-day month before Passover's month, under the name it takes in a Leap year when Adar I precedes it. It keeps the festival of Purim on its 14th day and is the 13th month when you count from Passover's month.
