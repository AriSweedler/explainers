Splats and Triangles

Two ways to draw a scene, measured against each other.

Triangle

Every picture on a screen is a grid. One cell of that grid is a Pixel, a tiny square that holds exactly one color. A 1920 by 1080 screen has 2,073,600 of them. A smooth shape cannot be smooth on such a grid. A renderer can only decide, cell by cell, which squares to fill.

The dashed disc is the shape we want, and its green fill is what the picture should hold. The blue cells lie wholly inside the disc, so they can honestly hold its color. The red cells have their center inside but their square crosses the rim. A Pixel holds one color, so each red cell is wrong either way: it over-fills at its outer corner and drops the green sliver beside it. At four across the disc is two sure cells and four rim cells. At eight it is 21 cells, 12 sure and 9 on the rim. At thirty-two it takes 349 cells, and 43 of them still straddle the rim. The red share of the column thins as the grid refines, but it never reaches zero. Every renderer in this article ends at this grid. They differ only in how they decide what each cell holds.

The shape a graphics card is built to fill is the Triangle, three corner points joined into a flat face. Three points always lie on one plane, so a Triangle cannot bend. A four-cornered face can.

Both faces start flat, one shade each. Lift the far corner and the three-cornered face stays one shade: its three points still share a plane, so it tilts as a whole. The four-cornered face breaks along a crease into two Triangles, and at full lift the crease angle is far from zero while the Triangle's stays at zero. That is why the card accepts Triangles and nothing else. One fixed procedure fills every one of them, and no input can break it.

The card never receives a shape. It receives corners. Each corner is a Vertex, a 3D position plus whatever extra numbers the surface needs there, such as a color or the direction it faces. The application hands over an ordered list of them. The card joins every three in a row into one Triangle.

The grey cursor is the card reading the list. Each row it passes drops a dot onto the canvas at the listed coordinates. The third dot closes the first Triangle. By the end of the list twelve corners have become four Triangles. The card joined them only because they were neighbours in the list. Start the reading one row later and the same twelve corners make three different Triangles; row 1 is never read and rows 11 and 12 have no partners, so they draw nothing. The card never saw a shape, only an order.

A whole object stored this way is a Mesh, a shell of Triangles given as a list of Vertices and a note of which three form each face. A curved object has no flat parts, yet its Mesh is nothing but flat parts. The only cure is more of them.

The dashed egg is the shape we want. The red crescents are the egg the fan has not covered, and the highlighted bar is their measured share. Three Triangles leave more than half the egg bare. Eight leave a tenth, and forty-eight leave a third of one percent. Each doubling of the budget roughly halves the red and buys less than the doubling before. The corners are shared: the rim corner belongs to two neighbouring Triangles and the center to all eight. Drawn one Triangle at a time the eight would carry 24 corners; the Mesh stores nine, as the count reads. The Triangle count is the budget, and smoothness is what it buys.

Detail is a different purchase. A brick wall is flat, so two Triangles cover it. The bricks are detail, and detail would otherwise cost a face per brick. A Texture, an ordinary image stretched over a Mesh so that each Triangle shows one patch of it, paints the detail on instead.

Both walls hold the same bricks. The left wall builds each brick from two edged Triangles, so four rows cost 36 and six rows cost 78. Its bar stretches with every row. The right wall paints the same bricks as a picture over its two Triangles, and its bar never moves. The picture's own cost, a 64 by 64 image of 4,096 cells, is paid once and sits fixed beneath them.

This is the whole strength of the shell: hard edges, flat surfaces and painted detail are cheap. Curves cost faces. Fuzzy things, hair and leaves and smoke, have no single surface to put a face on. We now have a Mesh: a list of Vertices, and nothing yet on the grid.

Pixel

A Triangle flattened onto the screen covers some Pixels and not others. For each Pixel it covers, the card makes a Fragment, a candidate value for that Pixel. A Fragment holds a screen position, a depth (its distance from the camera) and the color data the Triangle offers at that spot. A Fragment is not yet a Pixel. Two Triangles over the same Pixel make two candidates, and something later must choose.

The blue Triangle stays put over three Pixel centers and the orange one slides. Each Pixel whose center a Triangle covers gets a chip in that Triangle's color. Where both cover the same cell, the chips stack two deep. Apart, no Pixel has two candidates. Overlapping, two do, and six Fragments serve four Pixels. The fragments bar holds at six however the orange Triangle sits, while the pixels bar sinks beneath its dashed ghost. The red wash between them is work on candidates that a later test throws away; on top of each other, the two Triangles spend six Fragments on three Pixels.

The step that makes the chips is Rasterization, the fixed hardware rule that takes one Triangle already mapped to the screen and finds every Pixel whose center lies inside it. It starts from the shape and asks which Pixels the shape touches.

Each cell carries a center dot. A cell fills the instant the Triangle's edge crosses its dot. At rest the thin tip runs between two rows of dots along its dashed line, so the spike colors nothing and 19 Pixels fill. Lift the Triangle half a cell and the tip crosses a row of dots: spike Pixels light and the count rises to 22. A whole cell up it is 19 again, the same picture one row higher, with the rest position ghosted beneath. The plot at the right draws the count against the lift; it breathes between 18 and 22. A center can be inside only one of two Triangles that share an edge. So a Mesh of touching Triangles leaves no gaps along its seams, and it makes no doubled Fragments there.

A Fragment arrives with data, not a color. Shading, the work of computing the color a surface shows at one point from its own color, the lights and the view direction, runs once per Fragment. One flat Triangle can therefore show many colors. Its Fragments face the light at many angles when the surface they approximate is curved.

The outline of the Triangle never moves. Inside it, each Fragment carries its own facing direction, as a Fragment on a curved surface would, and computes its own brightness from the light. The bottom-left Fragment swings the most as the light turns, and its bar at the right swings with it; the grey beneath makes a dim Fragment read as dark rather than see-through. With the light upper right the brightness runs from 0.08 to 0.92 across one face; from below the whole face dims. The surface color itself comes from a Texture or from a color stored at each Vertex; the light does the rest. The cost of this step follows the Pixels covered, not the number of Triangles.

Two Triangles over one Pixel make two Fragments, and only one surface is in front. The card settles this with a Depth Buffer, a second image the size of the picture that stores, per Pixel, the depth of the nearest surface drawn there so far. The idea is from 1974. Wolfgang Straßer described it in his thesis; Edwin Catmull published it months later and gets the credit.

The camera sits on the baseline and depth runs upward. Surface A is fixed at depth 5 and surface B moves with the slider. Under a column A alone covers, the stored arrow is A's depth; under B alone, the arrow follows B. A column nothing has touched still holds the cleared 10. In the shared columns, B's write arrives and bounces while it is behind A, and still bounces when it is level with A, because equal is not nearer. Only once B is in front does the red arrow replace the blue one and the shared cell drop from 5 to 2.

The comparison itself is the Depth Test: it checks a new Fragment's depth against the number the Depth Buffer holds for its Pixel. The nearer one stays; the test discards the farther one. Nothing about that rule depends on the order the Triangles arrived in.

The red Triangle sits at depth 2 and the blue one at depth 6. Under the marked Pixel the stored depth is drawn as a bar: tall is far, short is near, dashed is cleared or lost. Draw blue first and the bar stands tall at 6; then red arrives nearer and the bar drops to the kept 2, leaving the dashed ghost of the depth it replaced. Flip the order: red first stores 2 at once, and the taller blue Fragment then bounces off it. Either order ends on the same picture. Solid shapes need no sorting, in any scene, at any count. The blobs pay for what Triangles get free here.

The Depth Test keeps the right color but cannot refund the Shading already spent on the wrong one. Overdraw is the number of Fragments the renderer colors for one Pixel in one frame, where 1 means exactly once. Anything above 1 is work spent on a layer that a nearer layer later covers, or that only tints what is behind it.

The grey background costs the frame one Fragment per Pixel, the grey base of the bar, 60 for 60 Pixels. Each translucent disc shades every cell under it again: the first disc adds 10, and the fifth adds 13 more. With five translucent discs the total is 114 Fragments for 60 Pixels, an Overdraw of 1.9. Make the same five discs opaque and the Depth Test throws the hidden Fragments away before Shading: 60 again. A translucent layer cannot be thrown away, because it changes the result.

All of this work has a deadline. Frame rate is the number of finished images a renderer produces per second, written as frames per second or fps. The 2023 paper that introduced the blobs counts at least 30 fps at 1920 by 1080 Pixels as real time. That is the bar every renderer in this article has to clear.

The renderer does not choose a frame rate. It has a time per image, and the frame rate falls out of it. The bottom row is one second; each blue block is the first 33 milliseconds of one image and the red tail is the time it ran past the deadline. At 100 milliseconds an image ten images fit in the second and twenty of the thirty slots above stay empty. At 42 milliseconds, a film's pace, six slots still go empty. At 33 milliseconds every slot gets an image and the red is gone. At 7 milliseconds, a gaming screen's pace, 144 images fit. Thirty images of two million Pixels is 62 million Pixel colors a second at an Overdraw of 1, and every extra layer multiplies it. That is the machine the card was built for.

3D Gaussian

A 3D Gaussian, or blob, is a soft see-through shape floating in space, densest at its center and fading smoothly outward, with no edge anywhere. A captured scene is nothing but a cloud of them: one to five million in the scenes the 2023 paper tested, under the name 3D Gaussian Splatting. There is no Mesh and no Triangle.

The two probes sit at the same offset from the center of the blob and of the Triangle; the dashed stem shows they match. The offset is measured in spreads, and the dashed ring is one spread. On the right the blue line stays at the top through one spread, where the blob already reads 61 percent, and then falls off the chart: beyond its edge a Triangle has nothing. The orange line is a smooth curve that keeps sloping down and never reaches the bottom. It crosses the red line, the smallest amount an 8-bit renderer can store, near 3.3 spreads, and the renderer stops there even though the math does not. A Triangle is a decision; a blob is a weight.

A round blob would be a poor building block. The Covariance Matrix is the 3-by-3 grid of numbers that gives a blob its shape. It lets the blob stretch by a different amount along each of three axes, and tilt those axes any way. Unequal stretches turn a ball into a needle or a flat sheet.

The dashed leaf is the target. For the ball the matrix reads 1 and 1 on its diagonal and zero off it. Stretch the long axis to three times the ball and the x entry grows to 9 while the y entry stays at 1: a needle lying across the leaf. Turn it through the tilt and the off-diagonal pair appears, always equal to each other, while some spread moves from the x entry to the y entry; at 30 degrees the blob fills the leaf. Stand it on end and the 9 has moved to the y corner. The footprint stays three times the ball's whatever the tilt. One blob is one leaf; a Mesh needs several Triangles to say the same thing, and the figure at the top of the article is that arithmetic repeated 36 times. A real blob has three stretches and three tilts.

Alpha is the fourth number stored with a color's red, green and blue, from 0 for fully see-through to 1 for solid. It is the fraction of whatever lies behind the color that the color hides. Alvy Ray Smith and Edwin Catmull added it to stored images in the late 1970s, and its first meaning was coverage: how much of a Pixel a shape fills.

The red edge of a Triangle crosses one Pixel. At 0 the edge grazes the Pixel's corner and covers no sample center, so the coverage rounds to nothing. At one half it covers 8 of the 16 samples. The stored Pixel on the right keeps only that fraction, a red at half strength through which the grey behind still shows by the other half. At 1 the last sample lights and the grey is gone. Every Triangle so far had an Alpha of 1.

The renderer must combine two translucent colors over one Pixel. The rule is Alpha compositing, Porter and Duff's rule from 1984 for laying a front color over a back one. The result is the front color times its Alpha, plus the back color times one minus that Alpha. Front and back do not trade places in that formula.

With the red disc in front at Alpha 0.6, the red segment of the weigh bar is the front disc's share of the overlap Pixel, as wide as its Alpha. The blue segment is what the disc behind keeps: 0.6 of the 0.4 the front let through, 24 percent. The pale remainder is the panel showing through both. Press the toggle and the blue disc takes the long segment; the two swatches hold both answers side by side, and the gap bar reads how far apart they are. At Alpha 1 the front disc owns the whole bar and the two orders differ by the full distance from red to blue, which is the Depth Test again. At Alpha 0.15 the gap is a 2 percent sliver and the two swatches are the same grey: order has stopped mattering. Nothing is faked here; the overlap is the browser applying the rule. For a front color FFF with Alpha α\alphaα over a back color BBB, the mix is

C=αF+(1−α)BC = \alpha F + (1 - \alpha) BC=αF+(1−α)B

Each blob carries its own Opacity, a single learned number from 0 to 1 that says how much the blob's center blocks what lies behind it. The blob's falloff fades that number toward the edges. The faded value is the Alpha that Alpha compositing uses at each Pixel.

Nobody types this number in. A fitting step draws the cloud from each photographed camera position, compares the drawing with the photograph, and nudges every blob's numbers until the two match. The paper calls that step training, and it sets this number per blob along with the center, the shape and the color. The number it stores can be any size; the S-shaped squash maps it to a value between 0 and 1, the purple dot. That one value scales the whole falloff: the center hides that much of the green behind and the outer ring about a tenth of it. The orange bell never changes shape against its dashed full-height copy, only height; at a raw value of −2 it is a low haze. Past a raw value of 4 the squash is already 0.98, so dragging to 8 moves nothing. A blob whose squashed value sinks under the red floor of 0.005 contributes nothing, and training deletes it; its ghost is all that is left.

A real surface does not show one color. A glossy table is white from one chair and brown from another. Spherical Harmonics store a color that changes with view direction as a short list of numbers per blob, 48 in all. The first three are the flat base color; each further group adds finer change across directions. The card never runs Shading on a blob. The paper's renderer reads the color for a view out of those 48 numbers once per blob, not once per Fragment.

The ring is the color the blob shows from each direction, and the blob reads its color off the ring where the eye stands. With the base color alone, the first three numbers, the ring is one flat green and the eye sees the same color from everywhere. One more band adds nine numbers and a slow swing of color round the ring. All four bands add finer patterns, the small rings beside each row, and the count reaches 48: a sharp purple highlight the eye finds near 52 degrees and loses a few degrees away. With position, shape, Opacity and these 48 numbers, a blob is 59 numbers. A scene of a million blobs is a file of 59 million. We have a cloud of 59-number blobs and no rule yet for putting one on a Pixel.

Splat

A camera looks at the cloud. Seen from it, each blob flattens to a Splat, the soft-edged oval patch of Pixels the blob covers on the screen. Its size and tilt come from the Covariance Matrix pushed through the camera's view. Matthias Zwicker and his co-authors worked out that formula in 2001 and 2002, for drawing scenes stored as points. A Splat fades from its center instead of stopping at an edge.

The camera at the left looks at the blob, a needle whose long axis is raised 30 degrees off the table. The two sight lines bracket its reach. On the screen the needle lands as a leaning oval, and the renderer visits every Pixel inside the dashed cutoff: 16 of the 64 broadside, 12 turned part way, 8 end-on, when the oval has shrunk to a near-circle. The Splat is the blob's counterpart of a Triangle's rasterized patch of Pixels, and its shape is the blob's shape as this camera sees it.

Along the line of sight through one Pixel, several Splats overlap, each with its own Alpha there. Transmittance is the fraction of light that still gets through every layer already stacked in front of a point. It starts at 1 at the eye, and each Splat multiplies it by one minus that Splat's Alpha. Each Splat adds its color times its Alpha times the Transmittance left when it is reached.

The ray from the eye carries the whole light budget, 100 percent at the eye. The first blob, with an Alpha of one half, takes half. Every later blob takes the same fraction of what is left, so the green remainder shrinks by the same factor each time, and the ray past the fourth blob is already faint. After four, 6 percent is left. Once the remainder falls under the dashed floor the renderer stops: with eight blobs it reads five and never touches the last three. The stop depends on Alpha, not on how many blobs exist. At Alpha 0.9 the light is gone after two blobs and six are never read; at Alpha 0.2 all eight are read and 17 percent still gets through. The real renderer stops when the stacked Alpha would pass 0.9999, a far stricter floor than the 5 percent drawn here. That stop is what keeps a million blobs affordable. Alpha compositing gives a different answer in a different order, so the renderer must know the order. A Depth Sort orders every blob in view from near to far, once per frame, on the graphics card. The figure shows why the order is not optional. How the card sorts a million blobs in a few milliseconds is in the paper's Section 6, in the reading list below.

Three blobs hang in the air, seen from above, and the camera slides along its rail. Each sight line's length is that blob's depth, and the number beside it is its sort key. Sliding the camera moves no blob and no screen position; it changes only the three lengths. With the camera at the left the red blob sorts first, and the magnified Pixel reads red over green over blue. Slide right and the green line becomes the shortest: the strip swaps two rows, green sorts first, and the swatch jumps to a new color. It jumps again when blue passes red and once more when blue is nearest. Each jump is the popping the paper warns about when two blobs trade places between frames.

Order dependence is the fee the blobs pay and the Depth Test let Triangles skip. It has two parts: a sort of the whole scene every frame, and every Splat that lands on a Pixel ahead of the stopping point. The second part is Overdraw by another name. On the paper's main benchmark the bill still came in at 134 frames per second, at the photos' own resolution, on one NVIDIA A6000. We can now draw both ways.

Fidelity

A score settles the argument, and the score starts from a photograph. A Ground-truth image is a real photo from a known camera position that the renderer tries to reproduce. A fair one is a photo the renderer never trained on. For the paper's main benchmark, the authors hold back every eighth photo and score only those. A model that memorized its training photos gains nothing. Our scenes have no camera, so their ground truth is the exact silhouette itself.

The grey silhouette is the Ground-truth image, 150 by 88 Pixels. The orange line is the render being scored, where the fitted blobs reach half cover. Each red square is one block of 8 by 8 Pixels, darker the more the render and the photo disagree there, and the readout sums the red. With four blobs the belly is covered and the handle and spout are solid red. With sixteen the body fills, and what stays red is every edge. At 128 the red has thinned to a rim along every edge: eight times the blobs bought six decibels on the blue bar, and the rim is the hard edge a soft blob never makes.

That blue bar is PSNR, peak signal-to-noise ratio: a number in decibels computed from the average squared Pixel difference between the rendered image and its Ground-truth image. Higher is better. The scale is logarithmic, so one extra decibel means about one fifth less squared error. It is the score behind the lead bar under the hero figure.

The triangles' red square and the blobs' red square have the mean squared error as their area, inside a dashed frame of 0.25. Each rung up the ladder is a tenth of the error, so a square a hundred times smaller sits two rungs higher. Drag the budget up and the square loses most of its area in the first few steps, its pale ghost marking the step before, while the marker climbs the ladder in even rungs. The bracket between the two markers is their gap in decibels, which is a ratio of areas. The slider sets the target budget; the label under each square is the count the fit used, since the fan over the teapot overshoots once the handle joins in. At four the blobs lead; at sixteen the blue square has shrunk past the orange one; at the end of the slider blue is a dot at 38 decibels and orange is two rungs lower. On the tree the order flips and no budget flips it back, because a leaf is one blob and at least three Triangles. The corner chart holds every budget we measured. Two caveats, both in the blobs' favor. We placed these blobs by grouping the outline's filled cells into equal patches, one blob per patch, not by fitting them to photographs the way the paper does. And a blob is 59 numbers to the nine coordinates of a Triangle's three corners, so a contest by bytes would shift every orange point to the right. PSNR has a blind spot. A blur moves every Pixel only a little, so the squared error stays small while the edges vanish. The Structural similarity index measure (SSIM) scores from −1 to 1, and reads exactly 1 only when two images are identical. It compares brightness, contrast and local pattern inside a small sliding window, so it punishes the blur that PSNR forgives.

SSIM scores the image one window at a time, 8 by 8 Pixels, and averages the 198 windows. The side window holds a vertical edge of the handle and the bottom window a horizontal edge of the belly. A blur turns the step in both windows into a ramp, so contrast and pattern both fall. A shift to the right pushes the vertical edge clean out of its window but leaves the horizontal edge where it was: at eight Pixels the side window scores 0, the bottom one 0.87. Averaged over every window, SSIM prefers the crisp shift, 0.687 against 0.569 for the blur, while PSNR prefers the blur by four decibels, 14.6 against 10.2. At three Pixels the two scores already disagree; sharp, both say identical. The paper reports both scores for this reason. Its trained blobs reach 27.21 decibels and 0.815 on the Mip-NeRF360 scenes, within half a decibel of Mip-NeRF360 itself. They do it at 134 frames per second instead of about one frame every 17 seconds.

Final words

Transparency alone does not decide the race. A hard-edged, flat world is a few Triangles and a Texture, and the Depth Test gives it visibility for free. A world of leaves, hair and glossy things is a cloud of blobs the renderer must sort and stack. It pays that bill because each blob says more than a face can. The deciding quantity is the total work for a target score, measured on the scene you have. The slider at the top measures it on two.

Glossary
Pixel ↩
One cell of the grid of tiny colored squares that makes up a digital image. Each cell holds one color as red, green and blue amounts, and a 1920 by 1080 screen has over two million of them. source
Triangle ↩
Three corner points joined into a flat face, the basic shape a graphics card knows how to fill. It is always flat and never folds over itself, so one fixed procedure fills every one of them. source
Vertex ↩
One corner point of a Triangle, stored as a 3D position plus extra data such as a color or a surface direction. The application hands the graphics card an ordered list of them, and the card joins them three at a time into Triangles. source
Mesh ↩
A 3D object stored as a shell of Triangles, given as a list of Vertices and which three of them form each face. The graphics card never sees the object, only the Vertices and the Triangles it builds from them. source
Texture ↩
An ordinary image stretched over a Mesh so that each Triangle shows one patch of it. It stores fine color detail without adding Triangles, so a flat wall can show every brick. source
Fragment ↩
A candidate value for one Pixel: the screen position, depth and color data that one Triangle contributes where it covers that Pixel. Several Triangles can produce one for the same Pixel, and a later check decides which survive into the final image. source
Rasterization ↩
The fixed hardware step that takes one Triangle already mapped to the screen and finds every Pixel whose center lies inside it. It emits one Fragment per covered Pixel, so it starts from the shape and asks which Pixels it touches. source
Shading ↩
The work of computing the color a surface shows at one point from its own color, the lights and the view direction. The graphics card does this once per Fragment, reading the surface color from a Texture. source
Depth Buffer ↩
A second image the size of the picture that stores, per Pixel, the depth of the nearest surface drawn there so far. The graphics card compares each new Fragment's depth with the stored one, so it remembers what is in front without sorting. source
Depth Test ↩
The check that compares a new Fragment's depth with the number stored in the Depth Buffer for its Pixel. With the usual setting the graphics card keeps the nearer and discards the farther, so solid shapes need no sorting. source
Overdraw ↩
The number of Fragments the renderer colors for one Pixel in one frame, where 1 means exactly once. Anything higher is work spent on layers that a nearer layer later covers or only tints. source
Frame rate ↩
The number of finished images a renderer produces per second, written as frames per second or fps. The paper behind this article counts at least 30 fps at 1920 by 1080 Pixels as real time. source
3D Gaussian (blob) ↩
A soft see-through blob in 3D space, densest at its center and fading smoothly outward, that is the building block of a captured scene. Its numbers give a center point, a stretch and tilt, how much it blocks what lies behind it, and a color per view direction. source
Covariance Matrix ↩
The 3-by-3 grid of numbers that gives a 3D Gaussian its shape: how far it stretches along three axes and how those axes tilt. Unequal stretches let one blob be a thin needle or a flat sheet, so few blobs capture spokes and leaves. source
Alpha ↩
The fourth number stored with a color's red, green and blue, from 0 for fully see-through to 1 for solid. Smith and Catmull added it in the late 1970s to say how much of what lies behind a color still shows through. source
Alpha compositing ↩
The 'over' rule from Porter and Duff's 1984 paper for laying a partly see-through front color over a back color. The result is the front color times its Alpha plus the back color times one minus that Alpha, so order matters. source
Opacity ↩
A single learned number from 0 to 1 per 3D Gaussian that says how much its center blocks what lies behind it. The blob's falloff fades it toward the edges, and the faded value serves as the Alpha in Alpha compositing. source
Spherical Harmonics ↩
A way to store a color that changes with view direction as a short list of numbers per 3D Gaussian, 48 numbers in all. The first three are the flat base color, and each further group adds finer change across directions, which Shading per Fragment never had to do. source
Splat ↩
The soft-edged oval patch of Pixels that one 3D Gaussian covers on the screen when the renderer flattens it toward the camera. Its size and tilt come from the Covariance Matrix, and it fades from its center instead of stopping at a hard edge. source
Transmittance ↩
The fraction of light that still gets through every layer already stacked in front of a point along the line of sight. It starts at 1 at the eye, and each Splat multiplies it by one minus that Splat's Alpha. source
Depth Sort ↩
The step that orders every 3D Gaussian in view from near to far, because Alpha compositing gives a different answer in a different order. The renderer does one sort of all blobs per frame on the graphics card, keyed by screen region and depth. source
Ground-truth image ↩
The real photograph from a known camera position that a renderer tries to reproduce. For the reported scores, the comparison uses only photos held back from training, so it measures new views, not memorization. source
PSNR ↩
Peak signal-to-noise ratio, a score for how closely a rendered image matches its Ground-truth image, computed from the average squared Pixel difference. Higher is better, and each extra 1 dB means the average squared error is about one fifth smaller. source
Structural similarity index measure (SSIM) ↩
A score from -1 to 1 for how closely a rendered image matches its Ground-truth image, exactly 1 only when the two are identical. It compares brightness, contrast and local pattern inside a small sliding window, so it punishes blur and missing detail that PSNR forgives. source
