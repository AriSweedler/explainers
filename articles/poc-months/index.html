Two Months

The Moon goes around Earth in about a month. Ask how long that month is, though, and you have to say what you measure it against. Let's start a clock at new Moon and watch two rulers at once. The dotted yellow line points at a distant star that never moves; the orange arrow points at the Sun and creeps along as Earth travels its own orbit. The grey Moon runs around the dashed orbit, leaving a trail behind it.

Move the slider until the grey Moon returns to the dotted star line. That takes 27.32 days, one sidereal month, the time for one lap measured against the distant stars: 27 days, 7 hours, and 43 minutes. But the orange Sun direction has crept ahead by almost a twelfth of a turn, so the Moon needs two more days to line up with it again. Keep going to 29.53 days and you reach one synodic month, the time between two new Moons: 29 days, 12 hours, and 44 minutes.

Notice that the small orange arc is the angle the Moon still has to close, and that the angle readout counts it in degrees. Every lap against the stars, Earth has moved on and the Sun line with it, so a synodic month is always longer than a sidereal month. The two rates subtract:

where Y\tok{star}{Y}Y is the year: how fast the Sun line itself turns. Put the numbers in and the Tsyn\tok{sun}{T_{\mathrm{syn}}}Tsyn​ the figure lands on falls out: 29.53 days. Every lunar calendar counts the synodic month, and twelve of them fall eleven days short of a year, which is where the trouble starts.

Final words

Two clocks, one Moon. Every calendar that follows the Moon has to choose which of the two it counts, and the choice sets the length of its months.

Glossary
sidereal month ↩
The time for the Moon to complete one orbit measured against the distant stars: 27.322 days (27 d 7 h 43 m).
synodic month ↩
The time between two successive new Moons: 29.530 days (29 d 12 h 44 m). Longer than the sidereal month because Earth moves along its orbit meanwhile.
